Abstract
D
ue to the large increase of Internet of Things devices in the last years,
the cloud has been proved unsuitable to deal with the high demand
of requests generated by them. To deal with this, fog computing was
proposed to provide closer computing services in a distributed manner, acting as
a middle layer between Internet of Things devices and the cloud. However, these
devices have small battery capacity and low computational power. Therefore,
strategiestoallocateresourcesefficientlyintheformofrequests, areofparticular
interest. These requests may have short deadlines and other requirements that
need to be ensured, usually in the form of Quality of Service. The most common
problem is known as the Service Placement problem, where the goal is to offload
a set of services while minimising some metrics of interest and ensuring that the
users’ requirements are met.
Another relevant problem is the Virtual Network Embedding problem, which
emerges naturally in Network Virtualisation and Network Slicing. In these
paradigms, the goal is to virtualise resources in order to allow isolation and
easier connectivity between physical devices with an abstraction layer. Thus,
there is a need to allocate a set of Virtual Networks efficiently, according to some
metrics of interest and constraints.
When the topology of the network changes or users’ move throughout the net-
work, Reconfiguration and Migration problems emerge in fog environments. Not
only users may move constantly, making it necessary to ensure constant con-
nectivity, but devices in the network may be unavailable, due to battery deple-
tion or overusage. In this scenario, there is a need to ensure a relocation of paths
and resources in order to ensure suitable Quality of Service.
In this thesis, the goal is to tackle the following question: how to model and solve
these hard resource allocation problems? For each of the previously described
problems, a Mixed Integer Linear Programming formulation and heuristic are
developed with multiple metrics to optimise and several associated constraints.
Since the problems are interconnected, a framework joining all three types of
problems is proposed as well. To evaluate each of these approaches, simulation
and an in-depth experimental analysis is performed. The heuristics are com-
pared to state-of-the-art approaches. The computational results show that our
heuristics are close to the optimal value and better than known state-of-the-art
algorithms.
Keywords: Fog Computing; Resource Allocation; Multi-objective Optimisa-
tion; Internet of Things; Quality of Service.

1.1 Motivation
With the expected use of 5th Generation (5G) technology in the next few years
and due to the high increase of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, fog computing
is becoming a promising paradigm to deal with IoT demands (Hu et al.[2017];
Yi et al.[2015]; Mukherjee et al.[2018]; Mouradian et al.[2018]). Fog computing
was first proposed byBonomi et al.[2012]. This paradigm aims to handle the
high increase of IoT devices and to focus on additional features, such as mobility,
that are not well-supported in cloud environments. Fog computing is composed
of different devices, such as local servers placed in strategic locations by Internet
Service Providers, IoT devices, routers, and switches, called fog nodes, which
receive data offloaded from user devices to compute or store temporarily services
or information. Depending on the scenario, it is possible to have several IoT
devices running simultaneously both in the user and in the fog domain.
Resource allocation problems are widely found on networking (Fischer et al.
[2013]; Sonkoly et al.[2021]). This set of problems arise when there is a need
to offload resource into the network to ensure some metrics of interest. The
most common resource allocation problem is the Service Placement (SP) Prob-
lem (Huang et al.[2009]), which is a combination of routing and task offloading.
The goal is to find a path between a user and a specific device on the network

and to decide where a set of resources should be allocated within the path. The
most relevant metrics of interest in fog computing are energy and latency, since
there is a need to ensure Quality of Service (QoS) and to decrease the energy con-
sumption due to limitations, such as battery powered devices. Furthermore, it
is necessary to avoid overusage of devices, since their computational capabilities
are lower when compared to the cloud.
Due to the heterogeneity of devices in fog environments, there is a need to
virtualise resources to allow isolation and easier connectivity between physical
devices by software as an abstract layer. Thus, Network Virtualisation (NV)
(Mijumbi et al.[2016]) has been proposed to decouple hardware and software.
Each network function can be thought as a set of virtualised resources to execute
a certain service. A generalised example of a network function is a Virtual
Machine (VM). Thus, when there are a set of services to be allocated to each
user, a Virtual Network (VN) is of interest. In this case, each network function,
called virtual node hereafter, has a set of virtual links to connect these functions.
Each link in a substrate network may be mapped to severalVN links, such
that the sum of bandwidth capacity of these virtual links does not exceed the
capacity of the substrate link. The same applies to the substrate and virtual
nodes. A step further is Network Slicing (NS), where not only resources are
virtualised, butthewholenetwork. Adivisionthroughlogicallayersisperformed
in order to improve security, resource usage, and configuration of the network.
However, severalissuesemergefromthese paradigms, suchashowto mapvirtual
components to the substrate components (Virtual Network Embedding (VNE)
problem (Fischer et al.[2013])) and how to deal with failures on the substrate
network such that reliability is improved.
Other concerns when dealing with resource allocation in fog environments are
the occurrence of failures on the network and unreachability of devices. Re-
configuration (commonly used inVNE) and migration (commonly used inSP)
problems deal with these scenarios. The goal is to change the location of paths
and resources in such a way that QoS is not affected and to be completely
transparent to the users when these issues occur.
From the described problems, a question arises: how to model and solve these
hard resource allocation problems? The next section provides the problem state-
ment and the contributions developed to solve the problems above.
1.2 Problem Statement and Contributions
Resource allocation problems are usually tackled as optimisation problems,
where several conflicting objectives arise, such as energy and latency, in the
context of networking. Thus, techniques that allow us to tackle these issues
and allow distribution of resources according to specified metrics are very rel-
evant. Chapter 3 shows that most existing works do not take both views into
consideration.
The goal of this thesis is to tackle resource allocation problems in fog environ-
mentsfromtwoperspectives: weconsiderfairnessinordertodistributeresources

according to a metric of interest. Fairness is considered as a model to minimise
the maximum deviation of a given metric, i.e., to limit the overusage of devices.
Moreover, we consider optimisation models that take into account multiple ob-
jectives simultaneously. In particular, the focus is on the notion of compromise
solution, which is a trade-off solution between objectives without any explicit
preference. In order to solve these problems, we formulate them as Mixed Integer
Linear Programming models and develop heuristics to solve them.
My contributions are focused on four problems:
1. Service Placement (Chapter 4): A Mixed Integer Linear Programming

and fairness of energy between devices. The goal is to use these models

2. Reconfiguration (Chapter 5): A Mixed Integer Linear Programming for-

and latency between devices. The goal is to use these models when a user

of energy and interference of resources/network slices in different time-

in each individual Virtual Network, such that the whole process is trans-

For each of the four problems, a simulation and an experimental analysis of
the proposed solution methods were conducted. The empirical results suggest
that the proposed models are able to decrease the overusage of devices while
providing compromise solutions. When evaluating the heuristics, the solutions
obtained are very close to the optimal, while taking a few milliseconds of running
time.

2.1.1 Fog Computing
Fog computing has several accepted definitions (Mukherjee et al.[2018]). In this
thesis, fog computing is described as a set of heterogeneous devices that have
communication and resource capabilities that improve the quality of connection
and to decrease cloud usage by acting as a layer between the cloud and users. It
can also mitigate issues that arise in connections between data centers and users’
devices. Thus, it is possible to ensure Quality of Service (QoS) requirements of
the users by decreasing latency (Hu et al.[2017]; Yi et al.[2015]). QoS is used
to describe the overall performance of a given service, where it is measured
quantitatively by observing some network aspects, such as availability, latency,
and throughput, to allow services with different priorities to meet the required
level of performance. For example, voice communications have different latency
requirements than streaming communications.
A basic network architecture that represents the Cloud-Fog Continuum is de-
scribed by Hu et al.[2017]. This architecture is composed by cloud, fog, and
users. First, the cloud layer contains high-performance servers, effective for large
computational services and storage capacity. In this layer, all the information
is saved permanently, contrarily to the fog layer, where it is only saved tem-
porarily and in low quantities. Considering its large computational power, it
makes possible the realisation of data analytics, relevant for discovering useful
information and supporting decision-making. It is also distributed by using sev-
eral clusters of data centers throughout the planet. Next, the fog layer extends
the cloud layer in a decentralised manner to provide more coverage, making it
useful to devices that need constant geo-localisation. It can be seen as a net-
work of gateways covering a larger area, where these gateways redirect traffic,
process data, and store temporarily data. Finally, the user layer is composed
of Internet of Things (IoT) devices, mobile devices, such as smartphones, and
other resource-constrained devices. Some of these devices are real-time, which
need a time-window answer, and offloading of services is also desired. A few of
the essential features of fog computing are highlighted as follows: to partition
geographical areas, to provide location awareness, to support end-device mobil-
ity, to provide real-time applications, to support different types ofIoT devices,
to provide access to the set of end-devices and computing nodes through the
wireless mode, and to collaboratively process a massive number of user services
requests.

2.1.2 Network Virtualisation
In order to allow usage of the same physical resource across several users,
Network Virtualisation (NV) has been proposed ( Chowdhury and Boutaba
[2010]). The goal is to combine network hardware and network resources into a
network software module, a Virtual Network (VN). Thus, the main objectives
are to decouple software and hardware and to allow flexible network function
deployment. It can be considered as a whole network, with virtual links and
nodes, or it can be separated into chunks according to their usage. In the lat-
ter case, there is a set of Virtual Network Functions (VNFs) that need to be
allocated, and a routing algorithm must provide a path between them.
By usingNV, it is possible to better manage the different devices on the network,
andtoimprovethedistributionofresources. Inordertomakethispossible, there
is a separation between the substrate network (the physical network) and the
VN (the logical network). Each link in a substrate network may be mapped
to several VN links, such that the sum of bandwidth capacity of these virtual
links does not exceed the capacity of the substrate link. The same applies to
the substrate and virtual nodes. By usingNV, it is possible to ensure that each
VN has only access to its own resources, since it is only aware of its virtual
components.
While the usage ofNV has increased in the last years, the development is still at
an early stage, with many open questions. As Internet Service Providers look at
the details of implementingNV and accomplishing its foreseen goals, there are
concerns about the usage of some of these goals and whether implementation
translates to the benefits initially expected. There are important unexplored
research challenges such as testing and validation, resource management, in-
teroperability, instantiation, performance ofVNFs, and others that should be
addressed.
One possible approach to implementNV is Software-Defined Networking (SDN)
(Bizanis and Kuipers[2016]; Xia et al.[2015]). WithSDN it is possible to separ-
ate the routing process from the forwarding process. The objective is to turn the
routing process programmable, in order to allow communication. A simple ref-
erence model is used inSDN, which consists ofinfrastructure layer, control layer
and application layer. The infrastructure layer consists of switches, routers and
other similar devices that are responsible to monitor the network status, and to
process network packets over a set of rules provided by the controller. The con-
trol layer acts as an intermediate layer for the application layer, where a series
of functions for the upper layer are defined to obtain the network status, change
the forwarding rules or tune the decision associated to the system. The applic-
ation layer accesses the control layer by an Application Programming Interface

and contains the applications that accomplish the user requirements. Hence,
it is possible to access different devices by the same Application Programming
Interface to provide easier configuration, to improve performance globally, and
to encourage the integration of new modules.
Since there are different network devices in the Cloud-Fog Continuum, it is
necessary to ensure that these devices communicate correctly, in order to provide
a seamless experience.
2.1.3 Network Slicing
Since services have their own set of requirements and priorities, Network Slicing
emerged naturally after Network Virtualisation was proposed (Afolabi et al.
[2018]). NS has been recently gaining momentum among an ever-growing com-
munity of researchers from both academia and industry. It has been also the
focus of different standardisation bodies (e.g., 3GPP (3rd Generation Partner-
shipProject), IETF (InternetEngineeringTaskForce)and ITU-T (International
Telecommunication Union - Telecommunication Standardisation Sector)). The
possibility to create, on demand and in a programmable fashion, cost-efficient
end-to-end network slices and dedicate them for the dynamic provisioning of
diverse services is seen as an important feature of5G. In this vein, efforts are
ongoing towards developing a5G mobile system capable of deploying network
slices of varying sizes and structures.
NS is a managed group of subsets of resources, network functions, control, man-
agement/orchestration planes and services at a given time, divided inslices. It
consists of 3 layers: 1) Service Instance Layer, 2) Network Slice Instance Layer,
and 3) Resource layer. Each slice acts as a logical network that may contain fog
and cloud devices, depending on the requirements of the services to be allocated
in the slice. This allows a higher level of heterogeneity, and the Internet Service
Provider has only access to its own layer, increasing security. In several5G
standardisation initiatives, such as 3GPP, ITU,5G PPP and NGMN, three dif-
ferent types of slices are considered (Barakabitze et al.[2020]): enhanced Mobile
Broadband (eMBB), where there is a need to ensure large bandwidth require-
ments, Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency Communications ( URLLC), where the focus
is to ensure low latency and reliability, andmassive Machine Type Communic-
ations ( mMTC), where there is a high number of users, yet their requirements
are lower than the other two types of slices.
By usingNS, future generation networks are able to provide a better distribution
of resources, due to the ease of creating policies that allow a higher control of
the existing services according to their requirements.

2.2.1 Metrics
In the context of fog computing, different objectives (or metrics) and require-
ments are of interest, for the problems of Service Placement (SP), Virtual Net-
work Embedding (VNE), and reconfiguration and migration. We focus on the
following objectives:
• Energy consumption: Minimisation of energy consumption of the users
that have resource-constrained devices or the fog nodes themselves, since
the fog layer may be composed of small devices without the possibility of
charging.
• Latency/delay: Minimisation of communication delay. Users may have
(near) real-time systems that need responses in defined intervals of time.
• Resource usage: Maximisation of usage of computational resources in the
fog nodes, which implies minimisation of cloud usage.
• Throughput: Maximisation of throughput. Usually, this is formalised as
the data-rate of a link or the used bandwidth by a given service.
• Interference: Minimisation of resource interference between devices. If too
many virtual resources are used in the same physical device, degradation
may occur. In the case ofNS, slice interference is also considered.
Note that some of these objectives may be considered as constraints. For ex-
ample, inNS environments, some slices need to ensure an upper bound on the
communication delay.
2.2.2 Routing and Flows
One of the main problems in computer communications is routing (Medhi and
Ramasamy [2007]). Considering a set of generic devices and links connecting
them, this problem aims to minimise the cost of communication between two
different devices, usually called source and destination.
A network can be modelled as a graph, where each device corresponds to a
vertex (or node) and the connection between each pair of devices (be it wired or
wireless) is modelled as an arc (or edge) (seeRavindra K. Ahuja[1993]). The
traversal of each arc may have a cost (or weight) and the set of arcs connecting a
source and destination vertices corresponds to a path. When the goal is to find
the path that minimises the cost between two vertices, we have the single-source
shortest path problem. However, this problem cannot ensure feasible solutions
when there are demand constraints, i.e., when there is a need to send an amount
of information between the source and destination, yet the capacity of each arc
— 13 —

CHAPTER 2. CONCEPTS DEFINITION
cannot be surpassed. In the context of network communications, that capacity
is defined as bandwidth.
The category of problems that include capacity arcs are network flow problems
(Ravindra K. Ahuja[1993]). The simplest and most well-known problem is the
maximum flow, where the aim is to maximise the amount of flow (or informa-
tion) sent through the arcs while ensuring that the capacity at each arc is not
surpassed. A visual example is to imagine a set of water pipes. Each pipe has
a certain diameter, the capacity, and anywhere where the pipes meet, the total
amount of water arriving must be equal to the amount going out. The entrance
of water corresponds to the source and the faucet corresponds to the destina-
tion. An extension to this problem is the minimum cost flow, where the goal is
to minimise the cost of sending all the required flow from source to destination.
In communication networks, this may be visualised as a certain device sending
a set of data packets that need to reach the destination device, but each link
has a limit on the data packets that are accepted in any given time.
In realistic environments, it is necessary to ensure several services at any time,
with multiple sources and destinations. This can easily be understood by con-
sidering a set of users with different requirements, such as streaming of a video
and data communications. Each user may be located in a different part of the
network and the necessary contents may be in different servers. This problem
is called the multicommodity flow problem (Ravindra K. Ahuja[1993]; Pióro
and Medhi [2004]). The multicommodity flow problem is a generalisation of
both maximum flow and minimum cost flow problems. While the maximum
flow and minimum cost flow problems can be solved in polynomial time, the
multicommodity flow problem isNP-hard (Ravindra K. Ahuja[1993]).
Finally, there are two types of flows: splittable and non-splittable (Pióro and
Medhi [2004]). In the splittable case, the flow can be divided through different
links. An example would be to send data packets through different routes and
join them in the destination device when all packets reach it (multi-path). In
the non-splittable case, the required flow for each source-destination pair must
be ensured by all used links. Thus, it only follows a single path to reach the
destination device.
In this thesis, we consider minimum cost multicommodity flows with non-
splittable flows. As it will be possible to see in the next sections, this problem
can be further extended to consider node and other link constraints, on top of
the link bandwidth constraints.

in two parts: allocate the resources in a given device of the network (task offload-
ing) and define which path is used from the user to that device (routing). While
the simplest version of the problem with no resource constraints is easy to solve,

2.2.4 Virtual Network Embedding
A similar resource allocation problem is the Virtual Network Embedding (VNE)

topology must be allocated instead of a set of services associated to a user. This
means that while in theSP problem, the source and target nodes are defined a
priori, in theVNE problem they are usually not fixed.

2.3 Fairness
Fairness arises as a topic of interest in many research areas, such as optimisa-
tion and decision-making. Fairness, in a communications perspective, means to
provide an equal sharing of resources with respect to devices/services (SHI et al.
[2014]).
There are several issues that emerge in a networking scenario. Each device
shouldbeabletogetafairchancetoaccessresources, bandwidthshouldbefairly
distributed, QoSrequirementsshouldbefairlysatisfied, andenergyconsumption
should be proportionally distributed. In networking, several techniques have
been used to deal with fairness (Nace and Pioro[2008]; SHI et al.[2014]).
Researchers have proposed fairness metrics to help identify underused commu-
nication channels or underused resources in devices (Jain et al.[1984]). Fairness
can be adapted to consider different metrics, such as latency or energy consump-
tion. We are particularly interested on fairness optimisation models which allow
to distribute the resources as equally as possible.
In the context of this thesis, the goal is to improve the resources of the devices
that get the least amount of resource or to avoid overusage of devices.

2.4.1 Multi-objective Optimisation

2.4.3 Heuristics
Since there are several relevant problems belonging to theNP-hard class, we
need strategies to obtain approximations to the optimal solutions in a short
amount of time. Heuristic approaches have been widely used in optimisation
and showed great performance with well-knownNP-hard problems, such as the
travelling salesman problem (Applegate et al.[2011]), from a set of cities, and
return to the original city. The idea behind these approaches is the one of ”trial
and error”. For example, we may use an initial solution, such as a randomly
generated solution, and the algorithm tries to find better solutions for the prob-
lem by employing several perturbations. Usually, this is performed until defined
execution time ends or the best solution does not improve after a defined number
of iterations.

different output.
In the case of fog computing, the devices used have less computational capabil-
ities than the cloud, and the solutions need to be generated quickly. Therefore,
greedy approaches are useful to solveMOO problems in fog environments and
will be considered in this thesis.

4.1 Description
The goal of this chapter is to provide aMILP model of the problem to solve
a Service Placement (SP) problem in fog environments and a heuristic. In this
work, a static two-layered network architecture, composed of a fog layer and a
cloud layer was considered, as shown in Figure4.1, based on the work ofSkarlat
et al. [2017]. The cloud is composed by computing units and storage, and a
cloud-fog controller. This controller allows to establish a communication to the
master nodes on the fog, which allows the cloud to receive the services placed
there and decide which resource units are used. Note that we do not consider
how the resources are placed on the cloud.

The fog is composed by a set of clusters, where each cluster behaves independ-
ently. This means that each cluster receives services independently and has no
knowledge regarding the load of the other clusters. Furthermore, each cluster is
connected to the closest cluster only, in order to avoid unnecessary delays, and
is composed of a master and a set of fog nodes. The master acts as a controller,
which decides the placement of the services that receives. There are four alloca-
tion possibilities: a) the service is sent to a fog node on the same cluster that is
able to compute the service; b) the master computes the service; c) the service
is sent to the neighbour cluster; d) the service is sent to the cloud. Each fog
node can compute, at least, one of the three following types of services: sensing,
processing, and/or actuating.
The following contributions of this chapter are as follows:
• A min-max Mixed Integer Linear Programming (MILP) formulation with
two objectives: maximisation of resource usage and fairness of energy con-
sumption.
• A heuristic approach to be used in online scenarios which provide results
close to the optimal solution.
For the evaluation, a set of applications was generated, where each contains
a set of services, with low, medium, and high deadlines. The approach was
evaluated according to the distribution of services throughout the fog and cloud.
The results show that the services are allocated fairly regarding their energy
consumption throughout the system whereas lower priority services are sent
to the cloud, while meeting the deadlines. Furthermore, the heuristic obtains
solutions that are 1% away of the optimal solution, in average.

4.5 Summary
In this chapter, a solution to the Service Placement problem is provided. A
static two-layered network architecture, composed by fog and cloud was con-
sidered. The fog consists of clusters, each containing a master node and a set of
fog nodes. In order to place the services, two objectives were considered: maxim-
isation of fog usage and maximisation of energy fairness throughout the system,
using a min-max formulation. For this, a Mixed Integer Linear Programming
formulation and a heuristic were proposed, that model both objectives taking
into account the constraints of the devices and the requirements of the services.
Both approaches were compared using a set of randomly generated applications
composed by different deadlines and services. While the formulation distributes
better the services regarding energy consumption, the heuristic distributes bet-
ter the number of services. Moreover, both approaches provide similar usage of
the fog.

6.2 Mixed Integer Linear Programming Formulation
In this section, aMILP formulation used to allocate requestedVNs on various
slices is detailed (a non-linear formulation (Mixed Integer Programming (MIP))
is presented for the sake of comprehension. The linearised version can be ob-
tained using standard techniques (Williams [2013]); see AppendixA.3). Since
Service Function Chaining (SFC) requests are a specific case ofVNs, the pro-
posed approaches focus on the more generalisedVNs. However, both types of
requests can be applied. Two different states of the network are considered:
current and future. Thus, this model contains two temporal windows. The
requests that arise in the first window are referred ascurrent requests, which
correspond to requests that emerge in a specific moment. In the second time

manner, we prioritise the current requests.

7.1 Description
In a smart event environment, with a conglomeration of users, there is a need
for computational and communication resources (Afolabi et al. [2017]). One
example of this environment is the Olympics Games, which is composed of dif-
ferent areas, such as marathon, cycling, martial arts, and others. It is assumed
that users in an area have a set of applications with their own requirements
(DOCOMO 5G White Paper[2014]; Frascolla et al.[2017]). To avoid degrad-
ation of Quality of Service (QoS), it is necessary to adequately distribute re-
sources, since each user needs to have its own slice of the network and resources,
whileensuringlatencyrequirementstomeetdeadlinesoftheusers. Theseapplic-
ations may be video sharing, health sensors or reception of information concern-
ing results of other events. Each venue may also have its own set of applications
and requirements, such as video monitoring and disaster detection.
Figure 7.1 shows a diagram of the proposed framework. It works as follows:
first, there is a user that needs a connection to a network with a set of services
to offload. The request is in the form of a Virtual Network (VN), which provides
the necessary resources for the user. In order to take a decision, the solution
method obtains a (sub)optimal solution that indicates where thisVN will be
allocated in the physical network. If it is not possible to allocate, then the user

is rejected. Next, the solution method for theSP on the users’VN is used to
efficiently decide where the services are offloaded in theVN. If it is not possible
to offload, then the services are sent to the cloud. In the case of a failure on a
node/link on the physical network, the reconfiguration heuristic is used, where
the users’ VN will be migrated to a new place to be usable again as fast as
possible. Otherwise, it is not necessary to perform any action.
Here, the topology considered in this work is described. The topology is split
into a set of clusters. Each cluster has its own substrate network to which
users in that area connect. Every time a user needs an Internet connection, it
requests a connection to the closest node and the system provides a uniqueVN
for communication and computing needs. Each node on this VN will need a set
of resources, such as CPU, memory and storage, which may be represented as a
Virtual Machine (VM) or container on a given node and each link will need to
ensure the requested bandwidth. Then, each user has access to a set of services
that may only perform communication or execute some processing along the
path, for example, video encoding before reaching the target device.
Figure 7.2a illustrates a possibleVN request from a user, with a set of virtual
nodes and links, while Figure7.2b represents a single cluster substrate network
and the corresponding mapping of theVNonto the network. For each link on the
VN it is necessary to decide which link(s) on the substrate network are chosen.
For this, we need to take into account the resources of the nodes and links, since
we assume that not only the link is mapped, but the associated nodes as well.
Moreover, we need to take into account connectivity, i.e., to ensure that the
existing paths on the VN are mapped correctly on the substrate network. In
the example of Figure7.2a, A−B is mapped into1−2 (red), A−C is mapped
into 1−4−5 (green) and B−C is mapped into2−5 (blue). The latency of
A−C is the sum of both links latency on the substrate network and both links
have to ensure that the requirements of the corresponding link on the VN are
met. On this work, it is assumed that each virtual link may be mapped into
more than one physical link. In some scenarios, it may be necessary to map one
virtual link in more than one physical link (A−C on the VN and 1−4−5
on the substrate network), in order to ensure that the VN request has the same
topology on the substrate network or due to lack of resources. We also assume
that each node is mapped only on one node on the substrate network, i.e., all
the necessary resources for a user must be on a single node.

7.3.3 Virtual Network Reconfiguration
In this section, we describe the reoptimisation techniques to be used when a
link or node fails. This implies that, when a failure occurs, it is necessary to
reoptimise theVNs that used those links/nodes. This is useful when there are
resource constraints on the substrate network and it may not be possible to
have a secondary path allocated for each VN or the secondary path cannot
be used. Despite being possible to use this approach to migrate partially or
fully VNs when there is overload of resources in a specific area, we focus only
on reoptimising the links/nodes where a failure occurs, since the proposedVNE
formulation/heuristic already provides a distribution of resources taking fairness
into account. If it is not possible to reoptimise theVN, the user is rejected and
the heuristic will reallocate resources on the next time window. For this, we
consider the following failures:Link failure and Node failure.
First, we focus on a single link failure. For this, we consider the following
approaches:
• If a substrate link (i, j ) fails and one or more VN are allocated on that
link, then a new path is found to reconnect nodei to node j.
• If a substrate link(i, j ) fails and there is no VN using that link, then no
action is performed.
• If a substrate link (i, j ) fails and one or more substrate nodes become
disconnected from the network, then the VNs associated are removed and
the heuristic reallocates them in the next time window.
Next, we focus on a single node failure. This implies multiple link failures. For
this, we have the following approaches:
• If a substrate nodei fails and one or more VN are allocated on the node
or the incident links, then a new path is found to reconnect the nodes. For
example, if there is a VN using pathj→i→k, then a new path fromj
to k is found.
• If a substrate nodei fails and there is no VN that is using that node or
the incident links, then no action is performed.
• If a substrate nodei fails and the network becomes disconnected, then the
VNs associated are removed and sent to the heuristic to be reallocated.
This allows us to have a fast reoptimisation on the network, i.e., obtain a good
approximate solution without the need to full reconfiguration. The time com-
plexity of theVN reconfiguration isO(tF ), where tF corresponds to the com-
plexity of finding a path.

7.3.5 Service Placement (Online)
Here, we extend the previous heuristic to be able to use it in a dynamic scen-
ario. We consider two extensions of the previous algorithm. First, we execute it
in time-windows, i.e., the heuristic is executed every time-windowtw with the
existing users requests. Second, we consider the resources being used as it is
considered on the scheduling constraints on the formulation, but with the ad-
dition of bandwidth. For example, if the bandwidth necessary to use a link is
superior than the available bandwidth, we also consider the time to wait until it
has enough free bandwidth if the time to go to another link is higher than the
waiting time until the resources are freed. The time complexity of the dynamic
version is the same as the static version, since the only difference is the execution
in time-windows and the increased time of waiting, which does not change the
performance.

7.5 Summary
In this chapter, a framework to provide Virtual Network Embedding, Virtual
Network reconfiguration and Service Placement of users services in a fog environ-
ment was proposed. For the Virtual Network Embedding problem, a Mixed In-
teger Linear Programming formulation and heuristic with two objectives was de-

a Virtual Network reconfiguration heuristic to be used when it is not possible
to allocate additional resources was developed. This allows to prevent users
rejection by reallocating only the Virtual Networks that were affected by the
node/link fails. Finally, a Mixed Integer Linear Programming formulation and
heuristic for the Service Placement problem, on the users Virtual Networks,
with two objectives was presented, to minimise overall energy consumption and
latency. It is possible to observe that the heuristics are able to obtain results
close to the optimal in most offline settings. In the online version, the Virtual
Network Embedding approach is able to obtain better distribution of resources
when comparing to an approach that does not use fairness. The dynamic re-

allocating a node/link disjoint Virtual Network for each user and the Service
Placement approach is able to consume less energy and has lower latency overall
when compared to the state-of-the-art approach.

Conclusions and Future Work
I
n this thesis, methods to resource allocation problems that arise in fog com-
puting are presented. Fog computing is an extension of cloud computing
closer to users since Internet of Things (IoT) devices are fastly increas-
ing and resource-constrained, which makes them unsuited to large computation
tasksorcommunicationstothecloud. Tosolvethis, fogcomputingwasproposed
as a layer in a network architecture composed of a user layer, a fog layer and a
cloud layer. Its most important characteristics are to allow low latency commu-
nication, temporary storage, low energy consumption, and to save bandwidth to
the cloud. The focus on this thesis is to optimise emerging resource allocation
problems, such as Service Placement, Virtual Network Embedding and Recon-
figuration/Migration with Mixed Integer Linear Programming formulations and
heuristics to obtain compromise solutions and taking into account optimisation
models that promote the fair use of resources.
Chapter 2 describes different concepts used throughout this thesis. Starting
from the description of the Cloud-Fog Continuum, Network Virtualisation and
Network Slicing, followed by the resource problems to solve in this architecture,
namely routing, Service Placement, Virtual Network Embedding and Reconfig-
uration and Migration. Finally, a brief introduction to fairness, Multi-objective
Optimisation, Integer Linear Programming and heuristics is also provided.
Afterwards, a comparison between different works is made in Chapter 3. The
aim is to analyse the most common metrics and objectives, how the problems
can be formulated, and what type of approaches can be used to solve these
problems. Considering the communications perspective, an analysis of the im-
plemented architecture, the types of tools and resources, and the applications
in realistic scenarios is performed. With this, several major gaps are identified
in the literature:
• In most cases, a direct comparison between the optimal solution and heur-
istic methods is not conducted. This lack of comparison may result from
the complexity of obtaining optimal solutions or the assumption that heur-
istic approaches are sufficient for practical applications. As a consequence,
the performance gap between these two types of solutions often remains
unexplored, leaving room for potential improvements and more informed
decision-making in the application of these techniques.

• The majority of existing methodologies in this field tackle the complexity
of multi-objective problems by applying some form of transformation that
reduces the problem to a single-objective version. This transformation
usually involves techniques such as weighted sum approaches, scalarisa-
tion, or goal programming, where multiple objectives are combined into
a single objective function. This simplification, while making the prob-
lem more tractable, may sometimes lead to a loss of the rich information
present in the original multi-objective framework, potentially impacting
the overall effectiveness of the solution.
• Hybrid approaches, which integrate multiple aspects such as Service Place-
ment, VirtualNetworkEmbedding, andReconfiguration/Migrationwithin
a unified framework, are generally underdeveloped. The lack of such integ-
rated solutions may be due to the inherent complexity of simultaneously
addressing these interconnected challenges. As a result, current research
often treats these issues in isolation, which could lead to suboptimal out-
comes when applied in real-world scenarios where these processes are in-
terdependent and need to be managed collectively.
• Numerous studies addressing the Virtual Network Embedding problem fail
to specify or assume any particular network architecture. This omission
can limit the applicability and generalisability of the proposed solutions, as
different architectures may present unique challenges and constraints. By
not detailing the underlying architectural assumptions, these works might
overlook critical factors that influence the performance and feasibility of
the VNE solutions in practical network environments.
• A significant number of studies in this domain rely solely on numerical
analysis to assess the performance of their proposed approaches, rather
than employing simulation tools for validation. While numerical analysis
can provide valuable insights and theoretical understanding, it may not
fully capture the dynamic and stochastic nature of real-world systems.
Simulators, on the other hand, can offer a more comprehensive evaluation
by modeling complex interactions and uncertainties, leading to a more
robust validation of the proposed methodologies.
• The majority of research in this area tends to overlook the incorporation
of realistic service values in their analyses, opting instead to use randomly
generated parameters for experimentation. This approach, while simpli-
fying the evaluation process, may lead to results that are not fully rep-
resentative of actual service conditions. The use of random parameters
can obscure the practical applicability and effectiveness of the proposed
solutions, as they may not adequately reflect the constraints, demands,
and behaviors encountered in real-world scenarios. Incorporating realistic
service values would enhance the relevance and impact of the research
findings.

As future work, the following proposals are described:
• To develop and propose tighter heuristics aimed at achieving improved
performance in larger and more complex environments. While the current
heuristics demonstrate a strong ability to approximate the optimal solu-
tion effectively in smaller settings, their scalability and efficacy in larger
environments remain uncertain. This raises concerns about their robust-
ness and adaptability when faced with the increased complexity, scale, and
diverse challenges presented by larger systems. By refining these heurist-
ics, it is possible to enhance their performance and ensure that they remain
competitive and efficient even as the problem size grows, thereby making
them more applicable to real-world scenarios where large-scale environ-
ments are common.
• To implement the proposed Mixed Integer Linear Programming formu-
lations and heuristics within real-world testbeds to assess their practical
effectiveness. While simulators provide a controlled environment to eval-
uate the performance of these algorithms, they often lack the complexity
and unpredictability of real network conditions. This gap makes it difficult
to fully understand how these approaches will perform when applied to ac-
tual connections and live data. By testing in real-world settings, we can
gain valuable insights into how well the algorithms optimise resource us-
age under varying and unpredictable conditions, thereby validating their
practicality and identifying any potential shortcomings or areas for fur-
ther refinement. One example is the OREOS project, where some Mixed
Integer Linear Programming formulations are being developed to be im-
plemented in MiniZinc and integrated into testbeds.
• To evaluate the energy consumption and time lag of the proposed heurist-
ics. In fog computing, minimising energy consumption and reducing delay
are critical objectives. Although the proposed approaches have shown
improvements in these areas, additional research is required to fully ad-
dress these challenges. For instance, a deeper understanding is needed of
how the execution of algorithms affects the energy consumption of indi-
vidual devices. Additionally, the impact of the time lag between making a
decision and the actual allocation of resources on overall delay remains un-
clear. Addressing these gaps will be essential for developing more efficient
and responsive fog computing solutions.
• To broaden the scope of connectivity to accommodate a diverse array of
devices, including both terrestrial and non-terrestrial entities, as we move
towards the deployment of next-generation networks. This expansion will
not only increase the sheer number and variety of connected devices but
will also significantly heighten the complexity of managing and optim-
ising these networks. For instance, aerial devices such as satellites, which
follow predictable orbits, may only be accessible during limited time win-
dows, whereas other devices like drones might appear sporadically and
unpredictably. Researching how these intermittently available devices can

of paramount importance. This includes understanding their potential
to improve latency, bandwidth utilisation, and overall network resilience,
