Abstract
H
ealthcare services and organizations rely on Electronic Health Re-
cord (EHR) systems to store, manage, and transmit patient data.
Consequently, EHRs play a crucial role in delivering high-quality ser-
vices while ensuring the privacy and security of patients’ sensitive information.
However, designing and implementing such complex systems with due consider-
ation for security and privacy concerns is far from straightforward.
This thesis proposes a Software Reference Architecture (SRA) specifically de-
signed for EHR systems, SRA-EHR, incorporating key privacy and security
requirements. The research begins with a comprehensive literature review to
identify existing privacy regulations, security requirements, and gaps in current
architectural solutions, highlighting the challenges EHRs face in meeting these
requirements.
The privacy-aware SRA-EHR is then developed using a well-established system-
atic approach, leveraging Privacy by Design (PbD). Its effectiveness is demon-
strated through a case study of e-SUS PEC, a federal electronic medical record
system in Brazil.
Additionally, a decentralized architecture based on blockchain technology is pro-
posed and implemented to manage sensitive healthcare data, enhancing security
and privacy in EHRs. This architecture demonstrates how blockchain techno-
logy can be leveraged to create privacy-aware EHR systems, improving data
integrity, security, and empowering patients with greater control over their in-
formation.
The outcomes of this thesis show that the SRA-EHR effectively enhances the
protection of sensitive patient information while ensuring compliance with reg-
ulatory frameworks such as Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act (HIPAA) and General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR). This research
contributes to the field by providing a structured approach to developing EHR
systems that prioritize privacy and security, ultimately fostering a more secure
and trust-based healthcare ecosystem. Future work will explore the application
of the SRA-EHR in diverse healthcare contexts, further refining its capabilities
to address evolving challenges in the digital health landscape.
Keywords: Healthcare Systems, Electronic Health Record (EHR), Privacy,
Security, Software Reference Architecture (SRA), Decentralized Architecture,
Blockchain.

Foreword
T
he rapid advancement of technology has transformed the healthcare
landscape, leading to the widespread adoption of electronic health re-
cords (EHRs) to enhance patient care and streamline healthcare pro-
cesses. However, this digital transformation has also introduced significant chal-
lenges, particularly concerning the privacy and security of sensitive patient in-
formation. In this context, the importance of establishing robust frameworks
that address these challenges cannot be overstated.
This thesis presents a comprehensive exploration of a Software Reference Ar-
chitecture (SRA) specifically designed for EHR systems, focusing on integrating
essential privacy and security requirements. The research undertaken herein is
timely and critical, as it seeks to bridge the existing gaps in current architectures
that often overlook the complexities of safeguarding patient data.
The work is grounded in a thorough literature review, identifying key stakehold-
ers, privacy requirements, and existing architectural solutions. Through a case
study involving the e-SUS PEC system, this thesis demonstrates the practical
applicability of the proposed SRA, providing empirical evidence of its effective-
ness in enhancing the security and privacy of EHRs.
As we navigate an increasingly digital healthcare environment, the findings of
this research offer valuable insights for healthcare organizations, policymakers,
and researchers alike. The proposed architecture serves as a foundational frame-
work that can be adapted and expanded upon, fostering a culture of security
and trust in healthcare systems.
I extend my heartfelt gratitude to all those who contributed to this research,
including my advisers, colleagues, and family. Their unwavering support and
encouragement have been instrumental in completing this thesis. I hope that
this work will inspire further research and innovation in the field of health in-
formatics, ultimately leading to improved patient outcomes and enhanced data

CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
1.1 Context
I
nformation security is an area of science that aims to protect data and
information systems from unauthorized access, use, disclosure, disruption,
modification, or destruction. Thus, implementing information security
measures is essential to protect both digital and non-digital assets across various
contexts, including personal, corporate, and governmental domains ( Hathaliya
and Tanwar, 2020). Healthcare is one of the most critical domains in which
information security is of utmost importance, as this sector deals with highly
sensitive patient data ( Appari and Johnson , 2010).
Electronic Health Records (EHRs) ( Evans, 2016) are digital versions of paper-
based patients’ medical records that store and manage health-related inform-
ation, providing centralized and real-time access to a repository of a patient’s
health data.
According to ISO/TR ( ISO, 2005), Electronic Health Records (EHRs) can be
defined as a repository storing patients’ historical, concurrent, and forthcoming
data in a digital format that can be accessed by multiple authorized users and
can be exchanged seamlessly and securely, supporting continuous, efficient and
high-quality integrated healthcare.
The primary concern in such integrated healthcare systems, which aim to provide
seamless and transparent doctor-patient relationships, is ensuring the delivery
of high-quality and effective treatment. This necessitates patients to frequently
share private and sensitive information such as medication details, progress re-
ports, vital signs, medical history, immunization records, laboratory data, and
radiology results with their healthcare providers. However, sometimes, they may
refuse to reveal their sensitive information that, when disclosed, may result in
social disapproval and discrimination ( Ghazvini and Shukur, 2013), representing
a significant challenge in the successful development of EHR systems ( Garcés
et al. , 2021). Thus, privacy and data security must be paramount considera-
tions in EHR system design and implementation. However, in the relationship
between healthcare providers and healthcare receivers, privacy has always been
a secondary concern despite its critical importance.
The idea of EHRs was first developed and used in 1992 ( Evans, 2016). Since
then, they have seen increasing adoption among patients, hospitals, doctors,
and other health professionals ( Wazid et al., 2018) as they bring several advant-
ages, including reduced healthcare costs and improved accessibility to health
records (Hussien et al. , 2019). On the other hand, using EHRs raised significant
concerns about patient records’ security and privacy ( Smaradottir, 2018). These
concerns affect patients’ interest in disclosing their health data, which can have
severe consequences. For instance, the United States Department of Health and
Human Services (HHS) estimated that approximately two million Americans
with mental illness did not seek treatment precisely because of privacy con-
cerns ( Yüksel et al. , 2017). Hence, nowadays, the widespread acceptance and
adoption of EHRs have been significantly influenced by concerns regarding pri-
vacy and security ( Evans, 2014), necessitating solutions that effectively address

these issues to facilitate the successful adoption of EHR systems.
1.2 Problem Statement
The advantages of digital healthcare systems, including Electronic Health Re-
cords (EHRs), have gained significant attention in recent decades. The literature
highlights several benefits of EHRs over traditional paper records, such as cost
savings, improved healthcare quality, advancements in evidence-based medicine,
enhanced data collection, and greater flexibility ( Jakhar et al. , 2024). However,
to fully realize these benefits, EHRs must meet specific requirements, including
data completeness, resilience to failures, high availability, and consistent privacy
and security policies. Despite these advantages, existing EHR systems are often
criticized as unreliable and compromising patient privacy ( Basil et al. , 2022).
Past breaches in the healthcare sector, which exposed 112 million records, un-
derscore significant security concerns ( Kshetri, 2017).
Given these challenges, the widespread adoption of e-health systems remains
in its early stages despite their potential. Security and privacy concerns are
the most critical issues, posing significant obstacles to the implementation and
acceptance of these technologies. In fact, privacy concerns profoundly impact
the definition and regulation of the doctor-patient relationship. Furthermore,
patients must share essential information with their physicians to receive ap-
propriate treatment. On the other hand, they may hesitate to disclose critical
details if doing so could lead to social disapproval or discrimination ( Ghazvini
and Shukur, 2013).
To address these critical issues, governmental bodies have enacted regulatory
acts, such as the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA)
of 1996 in the USA ( HIPAA, 2013b) and the General Data Protection Regula-
tion (GDPR) of 2018 in Europe ( GDPR, 2018). Additionally, Brazil’s General
Personal Data Protection Law (LGPD - Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pess-
oais), approved in 2018 and enforced in 2020, provides further regulation ( LGPD,
2022). These acts offer comprehensive guidelines on the storage, processing, and
security of medical data to mitigate fraudulent activities within the healthcare
sector. Nonetheless, cases of medical personnel unlawfully accessing EHRs for
personal financial gain have been documented, although the incidence of such
breaches has significantly decreased due to stringent legal actions implemented
by governments worldwide ( Sharma and Balamurugan , 2020).
While regulatory measures have been put in place to address security and pri-
vacy concerns in EHRs, these concerns remain significant ( Mishra et al. , 2024).
EHRs store highly sensitive patient data, and breaches can lead to identity theft,
fraud, and severe damage to patients’ trust ( Paul et al. , 2023). The complexity
of healthcare systems, the sensitivity of health data, its volume and widespread
sharing, and the risk of cyberattacks present ongoing challenges to the wide-
spread adoption of EHRs ( López Martínez et al. , 2023).
Moreover, the lack of reference design solutions or frameworks for EHRs is a

major barrier to their effective implementation. Standardized reference solu-

implementing these systems while ensuring that privacy and security concerns

Architecture (SRA) for EHR systems, with a particular focus on
addressing data security and privacy concerns . SRA acts as a funda-
mental yet abstract and generic architecture for designing and building a class
of systems, especially for organizations that are heavily dependent on efficiently
developing and maintaining complex and critical (i.e., business-critical or safety-
critical) software systems such as healthcare systems ( Martínez-Fernández et al.,
2017).
1.3 Research Objectives
Considering the challenges associated with developing EHR systems, as men-
tioned in Section 1.2, the main objective of this thesis is to propose a reference
architecture, named Software Reference Architecture for EHR (SRA-EHR), that
incorporates EHR security and privacy requirements within the architecture. To
achieve this objective, we outline a set of research questions that highlight the
thesis’s focus on enhancing the privacy and security of EHR systems through
the development of the SRA. The following research questions, developed to
accomplish our main doctoral objective:
• RQ1) How are the key security and privacy requirements outlined in ex-
isting regulatory frameworks for EHRs currently addressed by existing
solutions?
• RQ2) How can a software reference architecture for EHR be designed to
effectively incorporate the security and privacy requirements?
• RQ3) How can existing technologies, such as blockchain, support the im-
plementation of the proposed SRA?
The motivation behind defining RQ1 is twofold. First, it aims to review existing
regulatory frameworks (e.g., GDPR, HIPAA, and LGPD) to identify the key
security and privacy requirements for EHRs. Given that non-compliance with

these frameworks can result in severe legal consequences and a loss of patient
trust, the second objective is to evaluate whether existing solutions effectively ad-
dress these requirements. Answering this research question can provide health-
care organizations and providers with a clearer understanding of the necessary
privacy and security measures. Additionally, it offers an overall perspective on
how current solutions meet these requirements, highlighting the main gaps and
challenges involved. RQ1 is addressed in Chapter 4.
The motivation behind RQ2 derives from the foundational principle of privacy-
by-design, which emphasizes integrating privacy considerations into system
design from the beginning. Many existing EHR systems lack sufficient focus
on privacy, leading to vulnerabilities and potential breaches of sensitive patient
information. This research question aims to explore how to develop an SRA
that incorporates privacy-by-design principles, ensuring privacy remains a core
element throughout the EHR lifecycle. By addressing this research question, we
aim to establish a generic and practical framework that simplifies compliance
with regulatory requirements, reduces the risk of data breaches, and improves
the security of EHR systems. This framework can then be adopted by various
healthcare providers and organizations. RQ2 is addressed in Chapter 5.
The motivation behind RQ3 derives from the potential of leveraging well-
established technologies to facilitate the implementation of the proposed SRA.
Given the growing interest in blockchain technology within the healthcare do-
main, we aim to explore how blockchain can support the development of privacy-
and security-aware EHR systems. RQ3 is addressed in Chapter 6.

2.2 Health Information System (HIS)
Healthcare systems consist of various individuals, including pharmacists, labor-
atory technicians, physicians, nurses, radiologists, and patients. Hospitals typic-
ally manage, store, and maintain information collected during clinical meetings
in hospitals and physicians’ offices for extended periods to provide care and
follow-up for patients. However, due to the large volume of data, hospitals
sometimes face challenges in storing and managing patient health information
effectively (Nowrozy et al. , 2024).
Excellence in patient relies heavily on healthcare systems’ ability to electron-
ically collect, store, access, analyze, and transmit patient health data. ICTs
can significantly contribute to enhancing preventive care, improving healthcare
service delivery, advancing disease control, improving health management, and
supporting research initiatives ( Balsari et al. , 2018).
During the last decades, Information and Communication Technology (ICT) has
supported healthcare professionals in managing patient care information. ICTs
in the healthcare system have great potential to improve care in developed and
developing countries by improving access to critical information and facilitat-
ing care delivery ( Pai et al. , 2021). Healthcare institutions have invested in
information technology to enhance the quality of care and reduce operational
costs ( Berner et al. , 2005).
Moreover, a Health Information System (HIS) refers to systems designed to
support healthcare and, in essence, provides a foundation for decision-making.
HIS has four essential functions: (I) data generation, (II) data compilation,
(III) analysis and synthesis, and (IV) communication and use. The information
collected through HIS may originate from the health sector or other relevant
sectors. This data is analyzed, and its quality, relevance, and timeliness are
ensured and stored ( Dehnavieh et al. , 2019).
Several studies indicate that the implementation of an HIS enhances patient care

quality and safety by reducing medical errors. It also improves the institution’s
performance, reduces treatment costs, and, at the same time, saves resources
of medical institutions and health ( Ahmadian and Khajouei , 2012; Tan, 2008).
In addition, these systems can improve the readability of recorded data and
ultimately lead to user satisfaction ( Ahmadian et al. , 2015).
Most HIS systems are divided into two primary categories (though there are
additional types): (I) systems that manage individual data records, such as
Electronic Health Records (EHRs), and (II) systems focused on data collection
for decision-making (information creation and management), known as Health
Management Information Systems (HMIS). HISs consist of inputs, processes,
and outputs, all of which are influenced by determinants such as organizational
and technical factors. These determinants contribute to improved health out-
comes, directly impacting the performance of the health system ( Teklegiorgis
et al. , 2016).
Besides that, by enhancing awareness of current data protection and data-
sharing practices among individuals interacting with the healthcare system, the
use of EHRs for health research could gain broader acceptance as a standard
function of medical care ( Hutchings et al. , 2021). EHRs are critical assets in the
healthcare system and should be shared among physicians to improve diagnostic
accuracy and efficiency for patients ( Hashim et al. , 2021).
2.3 Electronic Health Records (EHRs)
Healthcare organizations are increasingly adopting new techniques for digitiz-
ing patients’ medical records. The volume of healthcare data has been growing
rapidly in recent years. Between 2020 and 2025, the total amount of global
healthcare data is projected to increase from 2,300 to 10,800 exabytes, repres-
enting an annual growth rate of 36% ( L.E.K., 2025).
In this context, the Electronic Health Record (EHR) system has emerged as
a solution for storing and managing patients’ health data ( Keshta and Odeh ,
2021). EHRs refer to electronic records containing comprehensive patient in-
formation, including their medical history, in a structured format (see Figure
2.1). This system enables easier and faster access and sharing of information
between different healthcare providers and allows access through linked sites
when necessary ( Shah and Khan , 2020).
EHRs are nowadays widely used to store and manage patient information both
within individual healthcare providers and across institutions ( Hussien et al. ,
2019). Several studies highlight the advantages of EHRs, such as reducing
healthcare costs and improving the efficiency of accessing and processing stored
records ( Nortey et al. , 2019; Yüksel et al. , 2017). However, the use of EHRs
raises significant concerns about the privacy of patient records ( Wilson and
Khansa, 2018). These privacy concerns can discourage patients from disclosing
their health information, potentially leading to severe consequences. The U.S.
Department of Health and Human Services (HHS) estimated that approxim-
ately two million Americans with mental illnesses did not seek treatment due to

persistent privacy concerns, which remain a critical research challenge ( Yüksel
et al. , 2017). Research also provides evidence that privacy breaches negatively

Unauthorized transfer or sharing of confidential health information can result
in a data breach. Privacy violations may also occur in other scenarios, such as

instances where access to patients’ health records is justified, such as by govern-

valid purposes. Despite these legitimate reasons, healthcare providers may mis-
use their access to health records, whether accidentally or intentionally, leading
to potential privacy breaches ( Cifuentes et al. , 2015).
2.4 Unified Health System in Brazil
This section explores the health system, particularly in Brazil, as the case study
presented in this thesis focuses on implementing the proposed Software Reference
Architecture (SRA) within the Brazilian context, specifically using the e-SUS
PEC as a primary example of an EHR system. This analysis provides a thor-
ough understanding of the challenges and regulatory requirements by healthcare
organizations in Brazil.
In 1988, the Brazilian Constitution established health as a fundamental right for
all citizens and a responsibility of the government. Consequently, universality

became a core principle, ensuring that all Brazilian citizens, without distinction,
have the right to access healthcare services. Universal health coverage in Brazil
was made possible through the creation of the Unified Health System (SUS -

Despite experiencing both successes and setbacks in implementing health pro-

2.5 Fundamental Privacy Concepts
The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) has
long recognized the vital role of privacy as a fundamental value and condition
for the free flow of personal data across borders. The main objective was to
provide a set of guidelines in order to protect privacy and, at the same time,
promote a free flow of information. Thus, to achieve these goals, the guidelines
establish set principles for member countries to use as a basis for national laws
worldwide. Thus, OECD Guidelines contribute significantly to the construction
of laws such as GDPR. In addition, the OECD Guidelines contribute signific-
antly to developing the 21st-century framework of privacy regulation (Horodyski,
2015).
Furthermore, one of the main objectives of privacy is to ensure that an in-
dividual’s health information is appropriately protected, favoring the flow of
health information necessary to promote high-quality health care and protect
the public’s health and well-being.
Therefore, privacy is defined as the “right to be reserved or maintained alone”
to stay away from the public domain ( Hussein et al. , 2022). The United Nations
General Assembly (UNGA) declared that privacy is essential to the Declaration
of Human Rights. Nevertheless, in this digital age, the term privacy has become
subjective and defined by each state or country ( Kayaalp, 2018). The privacy
of clinical data has been subject to many studies, and it has been not easy to
settle how much the data belongs to the patient, how much of it can belong
to health institutions, and whether the consent of the data owner is required
if the data is used for study object ( Richter et al. , 2019). Hence, one of the
fundamental principles that support the General Data Protection Regulation
(GDPR) is to increase the knowledge of digital citizens about consent to data
processing and use. This has important implications for data processing and
uses in the healthcare enterprise. To ensure that digital citizens are informed
when consenting to the use of IoT in the health field, it is imperative that the
needs of digital citizens are met to ensure that they have the information they
need to make informed choices. The term ”informed consent” came from the
community of physicians and was typically defined entirely by disclosure rules,
adequate understanding, and obtaining signatures ( O’Connor et al., 2017).

per, or oral. The privacy rule is also known as Protected Health Information

There is often confusion between requirements, standards, laws, rules, policies,
and guidelines, making it challenging to distinguish between them. To establish
a solution for healthcare systems, it is important first to define the meaning of
each term.
A requirement refers to a condition or characteristic that the system must meet.
In contrast, a standard is a set of specifications that defines compatibility criteria
for different systems.
Laws are regulations established by legislators and form part of the legal frame-
work governing a given jurisdiction. LGPD, GDPR, and HIPAA are conceptu-
ally laws. A rule, on the other hand, is a directive or order of behavior set by a
competent authority, with non-compliance resulting in a specific sanction.
Guidelines are general recommendations that provide direction but are not man-
datory or enforced. In contrast, policies are standardized requirements that
apply to specific areas or tasks, and they are mandatory. A policy is a set of
principles or plans designed to address specific situations, officially approved by
a governing body such as an organization, institution, government, or political
group.

2.7 Privacy by Design (PbD)
Several methodologies have been proposed in the literature to proactively incor-
porate privacy requirements into the system design process.
The Privacy Safeguard Methodology (PriS) (Kalloniatis et al., 2008a), pro-
posed in 2005, introduces a collection of concepts aimed at translating privacy
requirements into organizational goals during the system design phase. Addi-
tionally, it establishes a systematic approach for analyzing the impact of privacy
objectives on organizational processes.
The concept of Privacy by Design was initially introduced in the 1990s by
data privacy expert Ann Cavoukian ( McQuay and Cavoukian , 2010). Privacy
by Design aims to incorporate privacy and protection of personal data from
the beginning of developing products, services, projects, processes, practices,
technologies, and infrastructures.
Later, in 2009, the Privacy by Design (PbD) framework with seven fundamental
principles was published ( Cavoukian, 2020). These principals include i) Proact-
ive, not reactive, ii) Privacy as the default setting, iii) Privacy embedded into
the design, iv) Full functionality, v) End-to-end security (i.e., complete life-cycle
protection), vi) Visibility and transparency, and vii) Respect for user privacy.
PbD aims to safeguard privacy and empower individuals to retain control over
their information, resulting in a sustainable competitive advantage for organiz-
ations ( Semantha et al. , 2020).
The Privacy Safeguard Methodology (PriS) and Privacy by Design (PbD) meth-
odologies, while valuable in addressing privacy concerns, have limitations.
PriS, despite offering a structured approach, faces challenges in practical imple-
mentation due to its complexity. Analyzing privacy objectives and translating
them into organizational goals can be resource-intensive and may require spe-
cialized expertise. Additionally, PriS may struggle to adapt quickly to evolving
privacy regulations and technological advancements, potentially rendering it less
effective in dynamically changing environments.
On the other hand, PbD’s comprehensive principles for integrating privacy into
organizational practices encounter practical hurdles. Implementing PbD’s prin-
ciples across all aspects of product development can be challenging, especially
if privacy considerations have not been prioritized since the project’s inception.
Moreover, achieving a cultural shift within organizations to prioritize privacy as
a core value may meet resistance and require significant time and effort. Ad-
ditionally, while PbD aims to ensure privacy compliance, it may not perfectly
align with all regulatory requirements in different jurisdictions, necessitating
supplementary measures to ensure full compliance.
Despite these limitations, both methodologies are valuable frameworks for ad-
dressing privacy concerns in system design and development. They provide
organizations with strategies to navigate the complex landscape of privacy regu-
lations and consumer expectations, albeit with some practical challenges.

2.8 Reference Architecture (RA)
This section’s theoretical background contains the main topic embraced in this
thesis: Reference Architecture (RA). Software architecture investigators started
describing reference architectures earlier in this century. One of the first descrip-
tions was given by ( Kruchten, 2003), who states that a reference architecture
is “a predefined architectural pattern, or set of patterns, possibly partially or
completely instantiated, designed and proven for use in particular business and
technical contexts, together with supporting artefacts to enable their use. Often,
these artefacts are harvested from previous projects ” . This concept was suppor-
ted and improved by ( Bass et al. , 2003), stating that “A reference architecture
is a reference model mapped onto software elements (that cooperatively imple-
ment the functionality defined in the reference model) and the data flows between
them” .
Reference architectures often include essential knowledge about how to man-
age software systems architectures of a particular domain, presenting several
possibilities of use. Several purposes have guided its adoption and use, such
as creating architectures to support the construction of software systems, the
standardization of systems of a specific domain, the evolution of existing soft-
ware systems, and the construction of new reference architectures. On the other
hand, to develop a practical architecture, assignees must consider the systems to
be designed when setting goals, the context in which the architecture is defined,
and the goals of the architecture itself ( Angelov et al. , 2012).
Several approaches have been proposed in the literature to systematically cap-
ture security and privacy requirements. In the area of privacy requirements, the
Privacy Protection Methodology enables the elicitation of privacy requirements
during the software design phase, modeling these requirements as organizational
objectives ( Kalloniatis et al. , 2008b).
Additionally, the Modeling and Analysis of Privacy-Aware Systems framework
provides tools for modeling privacy-related requirements in systems designed to
be privacy-aware ( Colombo and Ferrari , 2012).
Other researchers have explored the connection between architectural patterns
and privacy requirements. For example, ( Muller, 2008) states that “a reference
architecture is created by capturing the essentials of existing architectures and
by taking into account future needs and opportunities, ranging from specific
technologies, to patterns to business models and market segments” . A notable
aspect of this definition is the emphasis on tailoring a reference architecture to
address both specialized corporate needs and customer-specific contexts.
Furthermore, (Laskey et al. , 2012) the authors state that a “reference architec-
ture models the abstract architectural elements in the domain of interest inde-
pendent of the technologies, protocols, and products that are used to implement
a specific solution for the domain” . In another study ( Angelov et al. , 2009),
the authors state that a reference architecture is “a generic architecture for
a class of information systems that is used as a foundation for the design of
concrete architectures from this class” . These definitions suggest that reference

4.3.3 Privacy and Security Requirements Addresses in the Papers
We also summarized the security and privacy requirements that must be met

These requirements, representing the most significant aspects of each article,
were extracted from all the selected papers to address our research questions.
Note that each article may encompass multiple requirements. The following
paragraphs provide a detailed discussion of these requirements.
Access Control
EHR systems must include mechanisms to control access in a manner that pre-
serves patient privacy by enforcing rules and restrictions on private data access.
Consequently, these systems are considered compatible with the requirements
set forth by HIPAA for accessing users’ health data ( Gardiyawasam Pussewalage
and Oleshchuk, 2016).
Emergency Access
EHR systems should enable access to patients’ Protected Health Information
(PHI) during emergencies. While access controls are essential, they must be
adapted to support emergency situations, which often require different rules
compared to those used under normal conditions( Farhadi et al. , 2019).

De-identification
De-identification enables the sharing of Protected PHI without compromising
patient privacy. While de-identified data may lose some utility, it should still
retain significant value for studies, medical research, and health policy assess-
ments (Grana and Jackwoski , 2015).
Audit Control
Auditing is a security measure that enables a healthcare system to provide se-
curity for data. Auditing means keeping a record of all users’ activities in a
way that makes it possible to track any information accessed, modified, or de-
leted ( Hussien et al. , 2019). At the same time, systems like EHR should offer
the option of traceability as a way of providing privacy.
Integrity
PHI that is improperly altered or destroyed can result in clinical quality prob-
lems, including patient safety risks. Thus, any tampering or modification of
data is prohibited by laws. In addition, data shared between entities must
originally represent information, that is, without modifications ( Gardiyawasam
Pussewalage and Oleshchuk , 2016).
Secure Transmission
Secure transmission refers to the implementation of technical measures capable
of protecting against unauthorized access to PHI transmitted through commu-
nication networks ( Hussien et al. , 2019).
Authentication
It refers to the implementation of mechanisms to verify the identity of a person or
system in order to ensure that the patient’s PHI is protected and has not been
accessed, altered, or eliminated by an unauthenticated entity ( Farhadi et al. ,
2019).
Consent
The Health Insurance Portability Accountability Act (HIPAA) and the
European Data Protection Act require patient consent for data to be shared
with third parties like insurance agencies and research organizations ( Jayabalan
and Rana, 2018). Healthcare users can terminate their consent at any time, even
before the consent has expired ( Zhang et al. , 2016). Laws such as GDPR and
LGPD pertain to the patient being given the right to delete his data whenever
he wishes.

Encryption and Decryption
Encryption mechanisms should be implemented as a way to protect and safe-

4.4.2 Emergency Access
Laws mandate that access to private patient data must be possible without ex-
plicit patient authorization in emergencies or life-threatening situations. EHR
systems must include a mechanism for medical personnel to access user data un-
der such circumstances, particularly when the user cannot manually grant access.
This technique, commonly referred to as Break-the-Glass (BTG) ( Jayabalan and
O’Daniel, 2017), ensures emergency access to critical health information.
The work presented in ( Eom and Lee , 2016) introduces a patient-controlled
attribute-based encryption (PC-ABE) method that allows users to control access
to their health data. This approach provides users with total authority over
their data, enabling them to decide who can access it. In emergencies, when a
patient is incapacitated, an access key is generated to grant the emergency crew
temporary access to the user’s private data. This key is unique to the patient
and is valid only for a limited duration, ensuring that inappropriate access to
the data is prevented once the emergency situation has ended.
4.4.3 De-Identification
De-identification is a technique used to remove or generalize specific pieces of in-
formation, making it impossible to re-identify individuals. According to HIPAA,
there are 18 types of identifiable data that must be removed to achieve de-
identification ( HIPAA, 2013b). Unlike HIPAA, GDPR does not suggest spe-
cific methods to “de-identify” data. Instead, the regulation states that data
can be “anonymized” or “pseudonymized” ( GDPR, 2018). Anonymization is a
technique that irreversibly alters data—such as through generalization or sup-
pression—ensuring that the information can no longer be used to directly or
indirectly identify individuals. Pseudonymization is a technique that replaces
names and other information directly identifying an individual with symbols,
codes, or other characters, effectively protecting the individual’s identity while
maintaining data usability.

4.4.5 Integrity

prevent employees from making unintended or deliberate changes that could al-

4.5.1 Privacy Challenges in EHR
The advantages of EHR systems have become increasingly evident in recent
decades. However, due to numerous challenges, the widespread adoption of these
systems in healthcare remains in its early stages. Security and privacy concerns,
primarily due to the high sensitivity of health data , are among the most
significant challenges these systems face. Privacy is a fundamental principle that
controls the doctor-patient relationship. Patients are expected to share necessary
information with their physicians. However, they may hesitate to disclose certain
details due to fears of social disapproval or discrimination ( Ghazvini and Shukur,
2013).
Understanding how effectively EHRs are protected and identifying the key
factors that contribute to their success is crucial. Over time, EHRs accumu-
late sensitive personal information that can significantly impact an individual’s
life and social status.
One of the main challenges lies in storing large volumes of data. Managing
and utilizing this vast amount of data without compromising privacy
has become a significant challenge for researchers ( Jayabalan and Rana , 2018;
Essa et al. , 2019).
Encryption methods are increasingly used to preserve data and ensure privacy.
However, they do not provide complete security. Moreover, the computational
cost of encryption techniques can be high for EHR systems or low-capacity
devices such as health trackers, IoT devices, and smartwatches that are com-
monly used by patients to monitor daily activities and measure personal data,
including blood pressure, heart rate, and electrocardiograms (ECGs). The activ-
ity log can also expose user behavior and identity due to the fixed address as-
sociated with the account. Additionally, to mitigate malicious attacks, such as
statistical attacks, healthcare systems must periodically update encryption keys.
However, this approach introduces storage and management costs for the key
holders ( Guo et al. , 2018).
Moreover, the use of blockchain as a solution for ensuring security and privacy
has become increasingly common in EHR systems. Smart contracts are being
used as a trend for access control due to their decentralized nature, a defin-
ing characteristic of blockchain technology, combined with cryptographic meth-
ods. However, challenges associated with blockchain include data storage on
the blockchain network, leading to issues of confidentiality and scalability. Data
on a blockchain network, which can include sensitive personal information such
as test results, medical history, or reports, is visible to all participants. Other
challenges include the lack of social acceptance and understanding of blockchain
technology, as it remains comprehensible to only a limited group of people. Ad-
ditionally, the absence of a universal standard to define network protocols further
complicates its adoption (Shahnaz et al., 2019). On the other hand, sharing EHR
information introduces challenges for users in understanding who has access to
their data. In real-world scenarios, some healthcare providers might gain access
to this data and misuse it, leading to significant privacy concerns. This issue is

4.5.2 The Main Privacy Requirements in EHRs
EHRs, like any other system, require minimum security measures, such as ac-
cess control and authentication mechanisms, which are the most frequently
citepd requirements in the researched articles. However, EHRs are responsible
for handling and storing sensitive patient information, including medications,
progress reports, vital signs, medical history, immunization records, laboratory
data, and radiology reports. Therefore, additional precautions must be taken to
protect the privacy of the information stored in these systems. Furthermore, en-
suring data integrity is crucial for EHRs, which is why encryption techniques
are consistently referenced in nearly all the articles. Audit control is another
critical component, as it is essential for tracking and enabling periodic reviews of
patient records. This helps ensure that only authorized individuals are accessing
sensitive patient data.
Laws and policies often reference various methods to ensure the privacy of in-
formation specially when shared with third parties, including techniques that
enable the de-identification of data. A common requirement in regulations
such as GDPR and LGPD is that users have the right to request the deletion of
their information once it is no longer needed for the original purpose. Addition-
ally, these laws mandate that users must grant explicit consent for the use of
their personal data and be fully informed about how their information will be
utilized.

et al. , 2019). Moreover, access control techniques that leverage cryptographic
methods, particularly the use of blockchain and smart contracts, have become
increasingly popular ( Sun et al. , 2018; Vora et al., 2018). At the same time, sev-

pseudonymization (Jayabalan and Rana, 2018) as ways to protect users’ privacy,
in line with the requirements of HIPAA ( HIPAA, 2013a) and GDPR ( GDPR,
2016a).

EHR systems have been developed through fragmented software implementa-

privacy requirements for EHRs, as identified and presented earlier in Section 4.4.

• Proactive/prevention: Privacy by Design (PbD) emphasizes proactive,

Among all the requirements, integrity and automatic logoff stand out as
critical preventive measures in the design and implementation of EHR
systems. Integrity ensures the preservation of accuracy, consistency, and
reliability of data and systems throughout their lifecycle. This means that
data must remain unaltered from its creation to storage and transmission,
free from unauthorized interference that could corrupt, compromise, or
damage it. Maintaining data integrity is vital for EHR systems to func-

secure exchange of information between health institutions depends on
maintaining the original state of data during transmission, preventing er-
rors that could lead to operational failures, communication breakdowns,
or even serious medical consequences.
Automatic logoff, on the other hand, is a preventive mechanism that ter-
minates a user’s session after a predefined period of inactivity. This feature
safeguards against inadvertent exposure of sensitive information by ensur-
ing that an unattended session does not remain open for unauthorized
access. Automatic logoff helps mitigate the risk of data breaches and in-
formation leakage, serving as a protective measure that aligns with the
principles of proactive security.

quiring action from individuals. Privacy protection is embedded into the
system by default, ensuring that it remains intact even if users take no ad-
ditional steps to protect their data. The GDPR Article 25 outlines specific
measures organizations must take to achieve data protection by design and
by default ( GDPR, 2018). It mandates that data controllers implement

data. The GDPR specifies that these measures must “take into account
the state of the art, the cost of implementation, and the nature, scope, con-

and severity to the rights and freedoms of individuals” (GDPR, 2016b).
Among all the requirements, de-identification and emergency access are
closely aligned with the principle of Privacy by Default. De-identification
involves removing or masking Personally Identifiable Information (PII) to
minimize the risk of unauthorized access to patient identities. This process
requires removal of PII such as name, email, phone number and suppres-
sion or generalization of quasi-identifiers, such as gender or age or other
data points that could identify a patient. If hospital staff later need to
access PII, stringent access control mechanisms must be in place, granting
access only to authorized personnel with appropriate credentials. All such
access must be continuously audited to ensure compliance. By default,
patient data processed for routine care should exclude PII, ensuring it is
inaccessible to unauthorized users. All these should be implemented in
EHR systems by default.
Emergency access similarly reflects Privacy by Default principles by en-
abling temporary access to patient data under specific circumstances. This
mode is activated during scenarios where patients cannot provide consent
at the time of care, such as life-threatening emergencies or other urgent
situations. Emergency access covers a broad range of use cases, including
both critical medical emergencies and routine patient care, where timely
access to electronic health information is essential. By default, access re-
mains restricted and is granted only in scenarios where it is absolutely
necessary, ensuring that patient privacy is preserved unless a genuine need
arises.
• Embedded in Design: This PbD principle ensures privacy is integrated dir-

functionality.
Among all requirements, access control and authentication must be integ-
ral parts of the design of EHRs. It is a mechanism that defines user roles
and permissions to determine access to specific resources, such as patient
data, and is a fundamental requirement for any system. Access control
can be implemented in various forms, including passwords, biometrics, or
Role-based Access Control (RBAC). Additionally, EHR systems must en-
force access control to safeguard patient privacy by applying rules and
restrictions on access to sensitive data. Therefore, access control must be
embedded into the system architecture during development.
Authentication is a key pillar of access control, but we have considered it a
separate requirement due to its importance in critical systems. Authentic-
ation implements electronic mechanisms to ensure that the patient’s PHI
is protected and has not been altered or destroyed without authorization.

5.4.2 Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA)
The mechanism for a Privacy Impact Assessment (PIA) was introduced with the
GDPR (Art. 35). It requires the data controller to conduct and document an
impact assessment before processing personal data. This assessment can cover
various processing procedures ( Semantha et al. , 2020).
A PIA is an evaluation carried out by an organization with access to confidential
and private data about individuals, stored or handled outside its system ( Hus-
sein et al. , 2022). Consequently, the organization must review its processes to
determine how they may affect or compromise the privacy of users whose data
it collects, maintains, or processes. The PIA process has been conducted by
various sub-agencies of the U.S. Department of Homeland Security (DHS), with
standardized methods for implementation ( Wairimu and Fritsch, 2022).
Roger Clarke defines a PIA as “ a systematic process for evaluating the potential
effects on privacy of a project, initiative, or proposed system or scheme” (Clarke,
2009). A PIA is typically designed to achieve three main goals:
• Ensure compliance with suitable legal, regulatory, and policy requirements
for privacy.
• Identify and assess the risks of privacy breaches or other incidents and
effects.
• Identify suitable privacy controls to mitigate unacceptable risks.
A PIA aims to identify and record the components essential in any proposed sys-
tem containing considerable personal information and to demonstrate how the
privacy risks associated with that system can be addressed. A PIA sometimes
goes beyond an assessment of a “system” and considers critical effects on people
who are affected in some way. Hence, the success of projects depends on meet-
ing legislative guidelines or policies about privacy requirements and community
expectations of privacy. Thus, if privacy issues are not adequately defined, this
can affect community trust in an entity and challenge the project’s success ( Se-
mantha et al. , 2020).

2022):

5.4.3 Privacy Risk Assessment (PRA)
The Privacy Risk Assessment (PRA) is responsible for assessing an organiza-
tion’s level of privacy risk. This module (i) considers the current cybersecurity
status acquired by the Cybersecurity Risk Assessment module and (ii) performs
the analysis of data processing operations to uncover potential privacy risks in
alignment with GDPR objectives and protect sensitive user data. More specific-
ally, it performs privacy risk analysis based on cybersecurity evidence from de-
ployed infrastructure sensors and the organization’s documented data processing
actions. For this, the PRA considers the interrelationships between Information
and Communication Technology (ICT) infrastructure assets that support data
processing activities, data sources, data subjects, and personal data and infers
the privacy risks that an organization may face due to vulnerabilities and threats
targeting it. The privacy risk assessment process results in a list of possible mit-
igation measures that security administrators can apply. These action measures
can only help cybersecurity decision-makers (e.g., information security officers)
define mitigation strategies based on computed privacy scores ( Papamartzivanos
et al. , 2021).

ing users to manage access to their health information. This method grants
users comprehensive control over their data and the ability to authorize
access. In emergency scenarios where the individual is incapacitated and
unable to access their Personal Health Information (PHI), an access key is
generated to facilitate medical teams’ access to their private data. Import-
antly, this access key is tailored to the specific patient and is time-limited
to prevent unauthorized data breaches post-emergency care.
3. Anonymization refers to the process of removing, modifying, or reducing
personal data (i.e., Personally Identifiable Information (PII) like name and
Quasi-identifiers like age, sex, address) in a way that individuals cannot be,
directly or indirectly, identified. There are several techniques to achieve
this, including removal or encryption of Personally Identifiable Information
(PII) and generalization, perturbation, suppression, and anatomization of
quasi-identifiers (Fung et al. , 2010; Emam and Arbuckle , 2013).
Examples of effective privacy models for anonymization include k-
anonymity ( Sangaiah et al. , 2023), ensuring each record is indistinguish-
able from at least k-1 others; l-diversity ( Zhu et al. , 2015), which extends
k-anonymity by diversifying sensitive attributes; and t-closeness ( Hao and
Ya-Bin, 2017), which maintains the distribution of sensitive attributes
within each group. These techniques help healthcare organizations de-
identify patient records, reduce the risk of re-identification, and securely
use data for research or analytics without compromising individual privacy.
4. Consent management processes involve obtaining patient consent for data
collection, use, and disclosure within EHR systems, ensuring transparency,
autonomy, and respect for patient privacy preferences. By implementing
informed consent practices, healthcare providers can empower patients to
make informed decisions about their health information, establish trust in
data-sharing practices, and comply with legal and ethical requirements for
patient privacy protection, fostering patient-provider relationships based
on mutual respect and confidentiality. Therefore, obtaining explicit con-
sent from individuals regarding using their health data is fundamental to
privacy protection.

In healthcare data management, safeguarding EHRs against potential threats
and adversaries is paramount to ensure data privacy, security, and regulatory
compliance. Adversary and threat models, along with Privacy Impact Assess-
ments (PIA) and Privacy Risk Assessments (PRA), play a crucial role in fortify-
ing EHR systems within a comprehensive framework, particularly in alignment
with regulations like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR).
A PIA aims to identify and record the components essential in any proposed
system containing considerable personal information and to demonstrate how
the privacy risks associated with that system can be addressed. Hence, a PIA
is appropriate for addressing privacy risks within a healthcare provider’s risk
management approach. Thus, while the holding out of a PIA is not an obligatory
legal requirement, the commissioner will often ask whether a PIA has been
carried out and will invariably require one when public sector organizations seek
to obtain a public interest determination, aim to establish a contract use of
information, or request certification.
Meanwhile, the Privacy Risk Assessment (PRA) evaluates an organization’s pri-
vacy risk level. It systematically assesses potential threats to data privacy by in-
corporating insights from the Cybersecurity Risk Assessment and analyzing the
organization’s data processing activities. This evaluation aims to identify vul-
nerabilities that could compromise compliance with the General Data Protection
Regulation (GDPR) and other relevant privacy frameworks. The PRA provides
a comprehensive risk analysis by leveraging cybersecurity evidence gathered from
infrastructure information and reviewing documented data processing workflows.
This approach enables organizations to proactively mitigate privacy risks, en-
hance data protection strategies, and ensure regulatory compliance.
Therefore, incorporating PIA and PRA into a framework proposal for protecting
EHRs involves identifying potential adversaries such as insider threats, external
hackers, and third parties and considering threat scenarios like unauthorized ac-
cess, data breaches, and data tampering. Mitigation strategies, including access
control, encryption, audit trails, user training, and incident response planning,
can be implemented to address these threats. In addition, by integrating PIA
and PRA, healthcare organizations can proactively manage privacy risks, im-
plement security controls, and ensure continuous monitoring to safeguard EHRs
and maintain patient data’s confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

5.6.6 Results and Analysis
To the best of our knowledge, this is the first SRA that aims to enhance privacy
and security issues in EHRs design using community software architecture
approaches. Well-established architectural models and approaches were followed
to design our SRA to answer these two research questions, as follows:
Question 1: “How to constitute a viable Software Reference Architecture (SRA)
for Electronic Health Records (EHRs) that systematically integrates privacy and
security considerations?”
An SRA for EHR systems is crucial for ensuring security, privacy, and efficient
data management within healthcare organizations. Therefore, a feasible SRA
for EHRs should consider the following key aspects:
• The SRA should incorporate robust security measures to protect sensitive
patient information. These measures should include encryption, access
controls, audit trails, authentication mechanisms, and integrity checks to
prevent unauthorized access or data breaches.
• At the same time, the SRA should prioritize user experience, providing an
intuitive and efficient interface for healthcare professionals to access and
update patient records.
• It should support various devices and platforms to enable access from
different locations and facilitate seamless collaboration among healthcare
teams. This integration ensures a comprehensive view of patient informa-
tion and promotes efficient workflows for healthcare providers.
Question 2: “Is an SRA centered on privacy and security a viable strategy for
tackling the challenges associated with ensuring the security and privacy of
these systems?”
By incorporating privacy and security considerations into the SRA, several be-
nefits can be achieved:
• Risk mitigation: A well-defined architecture that addresses privacy and
security concerns can help identify potential vulnerabilities and risks in the
system. It allows for the implementation of appropriate security controls
and measures to mitigate these risks effectively.
• Compliance with regulations: Many countries have stringent regulations
to protect patient privacy and ensure health data security. Designing an
architecture that aligns with these regulations, such as GDPR in Europe,
HIPAA in EUA, or LGPD in Brazil, helps healthcare organizations meet
legal requirements and avoid penalties or legal consequences.
• Confidentiality and data integrity: Privacy-focused architecture ensures
— 95 —

CHAPTER 5. A SOFTW ARE REFERENCE ARCHITECTURE FOR EHR
TO ENHANCE PRIV ACY AND SECURITY
that patient data remains confidential and only accessible to authorized
individuals. It includes mechanisms such as access control, emergency
access, anonymization, consent, tampering, and data loss.
• Trust and patient confidence: When patients trust that their health data
is secure and their privacy is protected, they are more likely to actively
participate in their own healthcare. An SRA that prioritizes privacy and
security helps build trust among patients and encourages them to share
accurate and complete information with their healthcare providers.
• Long-term sustainability: Considering privacy and security from the outset
when designing the architecture ensures that these aspects are embedded
into the system’s foundation. This approach reduces the risk of security
vulnerabilities emerging as an afterthought and promotes the long-term
sustainability of the EHR system.

and usable SRA focusing on EHRs. We also carried out a case study on public

6.4 Blockchain-Based Decentralized Architecture for
EHR
This section describes our proposed decentralized architecture based on permis-
sioned blockchain technology. From a high-level perspective, blockchain tech-
nology can be categorized into two main categories: permissionless and permis-
sioned. The main distinction between these classifications is the consensus pro-
tocol employed by the peer-to-peer network. A permissionless or public block-
chain allows unbounded access, allowing all users to participate in transaction
validation and block production. As a result of this openness, complete data vis-
ibility is assumed, which may lead to a degradation of security and privacy.
Therefore, a permissioned blockchain is a suitable solution for EHRs that re-
stricts access to authorized participants, significantly reducing the risk of unau-
thorized access and data breaches.
This controlled environment allows healthcare organizations to implement strin-
gent access controls and authentication mechanisms, ensuring only verified entit-
ies such as healthcare providers, patients, and authorized personnel—can view
or modify sensitive health data. Furthermore, using cryptographic techniques
within permissioned blockchains ensures that data remains tamper-proof and
immutable, providing a reliable audit trail for all transactions. This transpar-
ency fosters trust among stakeholders and aids in compliance with regulatory
requirements such as HIPAA, which mandates the protection of patient inform-
ation ( Tapscott and Tapscott, 2016).
The proposal relies on two solutions offered by the Hyperledger framework: i)
Hyperledger Fabric to manage various operations in healthcare systems between
actors (i.e., patients, doctors) and entities (i.e., hospitals, clinics); ii) Hyper-
ledger Indy to manage the identity of these parties. In particular, we use the
Hyperledger Indy configuration with Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs) and Veri-
fiable Credentials (VC) to connect the medical records documents with Hyper-
ledger Fabric an off-chain database based on InterPlanetary File System (IPFS)
is used to store data to guarantee blockchain scalability. We describe this rela-
tionship in detail in the following paragraphs.
Incorporating Hyperledger Fabric into EHRs offers notable advantages regard-
ing privacy and security enhancement. As a permissioned platform, Hyperledger
Fabric facilitates confidentiality through its innovative channel architecture and
private data feature. Within these channels, network participants establish a
segregated subset wherein each member possesses visibility solely into a desig-
nated set of transactions. These features suit EHRs because patients and entities
can exchange sensitive data regardless of the App used as the communication
interface (Hasnain et al. , 2023).
In addition, the Hyperledger Fabric includes an access control enforced through
a Membership Service Provider (MSP), which manages identities and ensures
that only authorized individuals can access EHR data ( Owivri et al. , 2024). For
instance, a doctor may be granted access to a patient’s records only if he/she

its controlling DID (i.e., refers to person or entity). In practice, DIDs are JSON
files materialized through public URIs that associate a DID subject with a DID
document, allowing reliable interactions related to that subject. In health, med-
ical records and charging authorization are documents in which the holder must
perform some authorization, especially when sensitive data must be shared with
other parties (i.e., clinics sharing patients’ exams with hospitals). The DID ar-
chitecture can provide mechanisms to manage the situation in this aspect.

mechanisms that can be tailored to the needs of the healthcare framework.
This allows the network to agree on the validity of transactions before
they are committed to the ledger, ensuring consistency and reliability of
the data.
• Scalability: The framework can take advantage of Hyperledger Fabric’s
scalability features to handle large volumes of healthcare data and a grow-
ing number of network participants. This is crucial for a healthcare system
that may need to scale rapidly to accommodate new patients, providers,
and emerging medical technologies.
• Modularity: Hyperledger Fabric’s modular architecture allows compon-
ents such as consensus and membership services to be plug-and-play. This
modularity enables the healthcare framework to be customized and up-
graded without disrupting the network, ensuring that the system can
evolve with changing healthcare and technology landscapes.
• Selective Data Disclosure: With the use of DIDs and VCs, patients
can selectively disclose specific information to healthcare providers or other
entities as needed. Hence, this selective disclosure feature enhances privacy
by allowing patients to control the extent of information shared, thereby
maintaining confidentiality.
The data contained within this digital repository may contain confidential pa-
tient information, including blood test results, X-ray reports, and other medical
records. Therefore, one of the biggest challenges related to EHRs on a block-

which challenges blockchain storage capacity. This challenge is exacerbated by
the fact that EHRs typically contain extensive imaging data and medical test
results, resulting in significantly larger data sizes than traditional blockchain
transactions, which usually involve minimal data.
Furthermore, researchers advocate for off-chain storage mechanisms, where data
is stored outside the blockchain in distributed or conventional databases. In this
approach, a hash of the off-chain data is calculated and stored on the blockchain,
ensuring the integrity and authenticity of the data. Various database architec-
tures, including traditional distributed or file systems like IPFS, can be utilized
for off-chain storage, addressing the limitations of blockchain storage capacity.
Additionally, the DID Document can connect with IPFS for efficient network

7.1 Summary of the Thesis
Patient privacy and data security are fundamental pillars of modern healthcare.
EHRs contain a wealth of Personal Health Information (PHI) that, if com-
promised, can lead to severe consequences for patients, including identity theft,
unauthorized access to sensitive medical history, and breaches of confidentiality.
Moreover, strict compliance with regulations, such as the General Data Protec-
tion Regulation (GDPR) or the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability
Act (HIPAA), is imperative to avoid legal and financial repercussions for health-
care organizations.
An SRA tailored for EHRs can be pivotal in addressing the challenges mentioned
above. Thus, this thesis focuses on presenting a software reference architecture
(SRA) tailored for Electronic Health Records (EHRs) with security and privacy
considerations, aiming to enhance the development of these systems. To achieve
this goal, we first perform a thorough analysis of the state of the art, focus-
ing mainly on existing regulations and standards, EHR privacy and security
requirements, and the existing solutions that address these requirements.
We concluded that existing SRAs are often too generic, lacking a specific focus on
EHRs, and fail to address the unique privacy and security requirements inherent
to these systems. Consequently, there was a pressing need for an SRA specifically
tailored to evaluate EHRs, ensuring that stringent privacy and security measures
are in place to protect patient information.
Therefore, we adopted well-established fundamentals and tools for designing a
system with PbD principles to propose our solution. We analyzed the classi-
fication of Reference Architectures (RAs), considering the primary security and
privacy requirements of EHRs, along with established architectural design meth-
ods. We propose a layered architecture for the SRA that incorporates privacy
and security. Subsequently, we derive five architectural views for the SRA: the
feature diagram, the context diagram, the decomposition view, the layered view,
and the deployment view. Each view presents the SRA software architecture
from a different perspective.
Moreover, we evaluated the proposed SRA through its application in a case
study. Specifically, we applied the proposed SRA and derived the application
architecture from a study focused on Brazilian EHRs. Our analysis highlights
the potential issues arising from the absence of an SRA tailored for EHRs,
particularly regarding privacy and security concerns surrounding patient data.
Through this case study, we demonstrate the practical applicability of our pro-
posed SRA in enhancing EHR systems.
We then proposed a decentralized architecture using blockchain technology to
improve the security and privacy of Electronic Health Records (EHR). This
approach enhances the management of sensitive healthcare data and gives pa-
tients more control over their information. The architecture includes privacy-
preserving features and strong access control measures to reduce the risks of data
breaches. A proof of concept demonstrates the feasibility of this decentralized
model, highlighting its potential to overcome the limitations of traditional EHR

7.2 Answer to Research Questions
In this section, we briefly address the main research questions of this thesis,
which were previously listed in Section 1.3.
RQ1: How are the key security and privacy requirements outlined in existing
regulatory frameworks for EHRs currently addressed by existing solutions?
We have analyzed the following regulatory frameworks: i) Health Insurance Port-
ability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) in the United States, ii) The General
Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) in Europe, and iii) The General Personal
Data Protection Law (LGPD - Lei Geral de Proteção de Dados Pessoais) of
Brazil. These frameworks collectively establish a comprehensive set of guidelines
to protect patient data and ensure electronic health records’ privacy and secur-
ity. We have identified the following requirements for EHR Systems as the main
privacy and security requirements: Access Control, Authentication, Consent,
Anonymization, Integrity, Audit, Emergency Access, Secure Transmission, and
Automatic Logoff.
Next, we have analyzed the existing solutions and found out that the exist-
ing EHR systems often struggle to fully comply with key security and privacy
requirements set forth by regulatory frameworks such as HIPAA, GDPR, and
LGPD. Many current implementations lack standardization and fail to integ-
rate essential privacy measures, leading to varying levels of data protection and
increased vulnerability to breaches. The fragmented nature of EHR systems
results in inconsistent application of access controls, data encryption, and audit
trails, which are essential to protecting patient information. Consequently, there
is an urgent need for a cohesive approach that aligns EHR systems with these
regulatory requirements to improve compliance and effectively protect patient
privacy.
RQ 2: How can a software reference architecture for EHR be designed to effect-
ively incorporate the security and privacy requirements?
We have designed an SRA for EHR that effectively incorporates security and
privacy requirements by adopting a Privacy by Design (PbD) approach, which
integrates privacy considerations into the architecture from the outset. The
process began by mapping privacy and security requirements to PbD principles.
Privacy Impact Assessments (PIAs) and Privacy Risk Assessments (PRAs) con-
ducted during the design phase helped identify potential vulnerabilities and
ensure appropriate protections were in place. Security Incident Management
(SIM) then addresses breaches effectively. Incorporating System Life Cycle Pro-
cesses (SLCP) ensures continuous evaluation and improvement of privacy and
security measures throughout the EHR system’s life cycle. By systematically
applying these fundamentals in designing the SRA-EHR, we are confident in its
effectiveness in building privacy-aware EHR systems.

The increasing reliance on EHRs in modern healthcare systems has highlighted
the critical need for implementing privacy and security measures to prevent

architecture to adapt to varying contexts and threats, healthcare organiz-
ations can better protect sensitive patient information.

logy was explored in this thesis, future work could also explore integrating
emerging technologies such as IoT and artificial intelligence, along with
adaptability. These technologies can help build more effective, efficient,
and secure EHR systems.

plored in future work is the development of user-centric privacy controls.
Empowering patients to manage their own health data, including consent
management and data sharing preferences, is essential for fostering trust
in EHR systems. This initiative will involve designing intuitive interfaces

B Code - Ethr-DID
The ethr-did helps create and manage Ethereum-based Decentralized Identifi-
ers (DIDs). These are identifiers for entities (such as individuals, organizations,
or devices) that can securely sign and verify digital interactions. This aligns
with W3C DID specifications.
The code in Appendix B is centered around the Ethr-DID library, which is util-
ized for creating and managing Decentralized Identifiers (DIDs). The objectives
of this code include:
1. Facilitation of Identity Creation: The Ethr-DID code provides a
framework for generating DIDs that can be linked to the blockchain, en-
suring that identities are securely created and managed.
2. Integration with Blockchain: The code allows the integration of DIDs
with blockchain technology, enabling secure transactions and interactions
within the EHR system. This integration is crucial for maintaining the
integrity and security of health records.
3. Support for Verifiable Credentials: The Ethr-DID implementation
supports linking Verifiable Credentials (VCs) to DIDs, which enhances the
ability to authenticate and validate identities in a decentralized manner.
4. Smart Contract Execution: The code is designed to execute smart con-
tracts that facilitate various operations related to identity management,
such as validation and revocation of DIDs, thereby ensuring a robust and
secure identity framework within the EHR system.
These objectives highlight the importance of both code implementations in
enhancing the privacy, security, and functionality of electronic health records
through decentralized technologies.
1. Key Features
• Create and Manage DIDs : Users can set up a DID linked to an Eth-
ereum address, which serves as the controller of the DID.
• Delegate Management : Add delegates (keys or addresses) that can act
on behalf of the DID for a specific duration. This feature is useful for
secure, off-chain interactions.
• JWT Support : Enables signing and verifying JSON Web Tokens (JWTs)
using keys linked to the DID.
• Compatibility with DID Resolvers : The ethr-did-resolver library
resolves DID documents created by ethr-did, enabling verifiers to check
authenticity and verify public keys.
