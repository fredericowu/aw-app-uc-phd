Abstract
In general, the industry is a competitive field, in which every second of production rep-
resents great investment of time and money. The automotive industry is no exception,
therefore, predicting the defects before they occur is progressively needed to reduce the
scrap rate and increase the robustness and reliability of the production processes. In
this context, the usage of machine learning models to predict defects in manufacturing
provides an enormous potential to improve such processes. This potential is unlocked
due to the ongoing digitization of the manufacturing industry and the ability to bring
together data from specific manufacturing processes.
Inarealworldscenario, smallandmediumsizedcompaniesdon’thavethesameamount
of data the big companies have, which can prevent the usage of machine learning mod-
els in this vital niche for the industry. This way, a collaboration in terms of data usage
could be applied to develop powerful and general industry solutions. But, although
ideal, this collaboration faces some challenges. Data privacy and data protection poli-
cies, for instance, prevent the companies to invest in a joint effort. The vulnerability
to external cyber-attacks is another aspect that hinders the mentioned collaboration.
Systems who share information endure such vulnerability, that can disrupt the com-
putational processes and even result in the theft of sensitive information.
Facing this impasse, the main objective of this thesis is to develop a framework to
predict defects in manufacturing environments with distributed machine learning, im-
plementingmechanismsforprivateandsecureexchangeofinformation, aswellassecure
machine learning training and updating. These goals will be achieved by using machine
learning algorithms to solve predictions problems, combined with federated learning ar-
chitecture. This combination will allow the training of machine learning models to be
performed in the worker nodes, i.e. manufacturing companies. This means that the
v

vi
machine learning algorithms are processed locally on a hardware device, keeping the
data safe and private, and only sharing the knowledge obtained, via secure channel, to
the main centralized model. The parameters, also named knowledge/weights, are then
averaged and updated. However, despite federated learning default privacy protection,
there are still concerns regarding data security as the federated learning approach can
be vulnerable to cyber-attacks. The secure aspect of the framework is obtained by
combining the federated learning method with signed digital envelope assuring data
integrity, confidentiality, non-repudiation, and authenticity.
To demonstrate usefulness and effectiveness, the framework was used to predict defects
in production line samples under cyber-attack events and the results compared with a
baseline model performance obtained from the original data. The proposed framework
was shown to be competitive with baseline model, achieving its goals to be compu-
tationally efficient, cheap, easy to implement and to be a general protection against
the various cyber-attacks. Thus, more investigation is needed, for instance, to bet-
ter understand the impact and effectiveness of the current framework against different
threats and to extend the current framework protection to in-memory processes. All
data used in the experiments of this thesis was created by finite element simulations,
using the in-house code DD3IMP, devoted to sheet metal forming processes, developed
and continuously updated in CEMMPRE.
Keywords: Federated Learning; Digital Envelope; Machine Learning; Defect Predic-

prever defeitos na produção oferece um enorme potencial para melhorar tais processos.
Esse potential é liberado devido à digitalização contínua da indústria transformadora
e à capacidade de reunir dados de processos de produção específicos.
Num cenário do mundo real, as pequenas e médias empresas não possuem a mesma
quantidade de dados que as grandes empresas possuem, o que pode impedir a utiliza-

1.1 Motivation and Problem Statement
Sheet metal forming is a manufacturing process widely used in the production of metal
components for the most diverse industries, namely the automotive, naval, aeronautical
and machinery industries. In this process, metal sheets are plastically deformed into a
desired shape by the action of forming tools, which typically consist of a punch, a die
and a blank holder, where firstly, a blank (i.e., non-deformed metal sheet) is pressed
into the die by the motion of the punch to obtain the desired shape; the flow of the
sheet material into the die is typically controlled with a blank holder [Hattalli and
Srivatsa, 2018].

In general, sheet metal forming processes allow obtaining high quality components
with high cadence and low cost. However, the formed components are often prone
to the occurrence of defects, which can make the overall procedure very costly in the
end. The numerous variables involved in forming processes, related with the material
properties, tooling geometry and process parameters makes it difficult for engineers
to accurately predict the occurrence of forming problems, such as cracking, localized
necking, springback, among others [Firat, 2007]. The apparently random occurrence
of forming defects due to sources of scatter mentioned above is of paramount con-
cern because the ever-increasing competitiveness in the automotive and aeronautical
industries has demanded very high quality and robustness requirements, particularly
in components that have a direct impact on occupant safety.
Machine learning (ML) techniques may aid to reduce the occurrence of these defects,
since, particularly, they can be trained with available data for building accurate defects
prediction models. The rationale is that the models can generalize well in unseen data
and successfully identify and check such defect patterns. However, it’s worth noting
that the effectiveness of machine learning techniques depends on various factors, feature
selection, model architecture, and the complexity of the underlying processes. Addi-
tionally, machine learning models may require continuous refinement and validation to
ensure their accuracy and reliability in real-world manufacturing environments.
Among the success factor of a machine learning model, two of the most important
are the quality and quantity of available data, where most of the time it is required a
substantial amount of data to generate accurate models. This necessity raises another
problem, in that mostly only the large size companies have the luxury to have the
requireddatatofeedmachinelearningmodelsinordertosolvetheirproductionfailures.
Conversely, the SMEs companies usually are not able to produce enough data to be
analysed, and therefore are not able to build machine learning models by their own.
One possible solution would be the collaboration among manufacturing companies,
where they would share their data to create a common solution to be used by them.
The first major obstacle of collaboration in manufacturing is the data privacy and
data security policies of each company and their desire to not share their sensitive
information with other companies. The vast majority of machine learning methods

2.1 Sheet Metal Forming
Sheet metal forming is a cost-effective manufacturing method that enables the mass
production of components. It finds extensive application in sectors like automotive and
aerospace due to its efficiency. In particular, the automotive industry thrives on innova-
tion to ensure profitability while meeting demanding quality, safety, and environmental
standards. In Europe, especially in Portugal, the automotive sector holds significant
importance, boasting over 400 companies specializing in component production. Ap-
proximately 8 percent of their business volume is attributed to metallic components
[Marques et al., 2022]. The sheet metal forming consists in the appliance of specific
forming tools, such as a punch, a die and a blank holder, during the manufacturing
process of metal components in order to achieve a controlled deformation of the metal
sheets into the final desired shape that will be used by the industry [Hattalli and Sri-
vatsa, 2018]. The whole process typically result in minimal waste and quickly achieve
the final part’s shape, often with just one press stroke or a few. Consequently, sheet
forming has the potential to save both energy and materials, particularly in medium to
large production runs, where tool expenses can be readily recouped [Boljanovic, 2004].
Rising costs of materials, energy, and labor underscore the need for sheet metal forming

2.1.1 Defects in Forming Process
In general, sheet metal forming processes, including deep drawing, allow obtaining
high quality components with high cadence and low cost; however, the variability
inherent to mechanical properties, tool geometry and process parameters makes formed
components often prone to defects, which can make the overall procedure very costly
[Firat, 2007]. As already mentioned, the occurrence of these defects can be considered
random due to its dependency on many factors associated with the raw material used,
the deformations applied to the material at each manufacturing stage and the sequence
of operations used. There is a high level of complexity attached on these occurrences,
which is difficult to explain and characterizes its random aspect.
Manufacturing process can drastically affect the behavior of the component in the cur-
rent and subsequent forming stages and, consequently, the final quality of the stamped
products. One factor that aggravates the incidence of defects is the force applied by
the blank holders. When the applied force is too strong, that can result in the thinning
and deterioration of the metal at a specific location. Excessive pressure in securing the
material hinders its ability to slide and conform to the profile curvature, potentially
initiating ruptures in the inner region [Ikumapayi et al., 2022]. On the other hand,
the insufficient blank holder force (BHF), in combination with material flow resistance
along the die orifice distribution at the depth of the stretch, can contribute to the
appearance ofwrinkles [Wang and Cao, 2000]. Another example is the hardness of the
blank that can influence in the appearance of lumps and subsequentlyscratches in the
surface of a sheet metal when the die comes in contact with it [Ikumapayi et al., 2022].
The raw material behavior during the forming process is not only influenced by the
manufacturing process but also it is critically influenced by its microstructure and

2.2.3 Algorithm Evaluation
Certainly, there are many algorithms that can be used to solve the same problem and
because of that, the correct use of model evaluation, model selection, and algorithm
selection techniques is vital in machine learning. The general goal of an algorithm
evaluation is to assess its performance, which in a supervised classification problem
translates ashow good is the generalization performance of a given model, or in other
words, how good is a given model predicting accurately in unseen data. This evalua-
tion helps in determining whether a model has learned the underlying patterns in the
training data and can make accurate predictions on new, unseen data, and not only
memorizing the training data, preventing a good generalization performance [Raschka,

2018].
However, model evaluation is part of an iterative process because usually the optimal
model performance is achieved by hyperparameter tuning, that involves experimenta-
tion. This means that using different model’s hyperparameter over a training dataset
will result in different models results. In this case, the evaluation process provides feed-
back on how different the hyperparameter settings impact the model’s performance,
guiding the process of hyperparameter tuning, that can be understood asto increase
the predictive performance by tweaking the learning algorithm and select the optimal
performing model from a given hypothesis space[Raschka, 2018].
As mentioned before, several algorithms can be suitable to solve one problem and it is
necessary to compare different algorithms to each other, in terms of performance,to
identify the ML algorithm that is best suited for the problem at hand[Raschka, 2018].
There are different techniques, related to data manipulation, to be used to achieve the
goals mentioned above, that include:
• Holdout: Arguably the most common and simpler technique, consists in split
the data in two datasets (train and test), fitting a model to the training data and
evaluating the model’s performance with the testing data.
• Stratification: Split and resample the data, when one class significantly out-
numbers the other, to ensure that the distribution of classes in the training and
testing datasets remains representative of the original dataset.
• Cross-validation: Divide the dataset into multiple subsets or folds, train the
model on some selected subsets and then tested on the remaining ones, repeating
this process several times, calculating and averaging the performance metrics over
all iterations to provide a more robust evaluation of the model.

2.3 Distributing Learning
In recent years, it was observed a large increase in datasets size that challenges ma-
chine’s storage and computation capabilities to use such data for machine learning
training. Commonly, the ML training paradigm consists in store all data in one place,
or node, and it is called centralized learning. The fact remains that training a ML
model can be computationally demanding and the training can take an impractically
long time on a single machine but training these models using more than one worker
node can reduce drastically the training time. For this reason, an approach for dis-
tributed training is used. The main idea behind this computing paradigm is to run
tasks in parallel instead of serially, as it would happen in a single machine [Campos
et al., 2017]. There are two approaches to achieve distributed learning: Data Paral-
lelism and Model Parallelism.

In this approach, the dataset is divided into multiple subsets and distributed among
different computing resources. The computing resources are basically worker machines
that contains a copy of the entire model and process different subsets of the training
dataset. Each worker machine will independently train their models and combine the
models’ parameters (or models’ weights) in the end of the process (Figure 2.16) [Liu
et al., 2022a]. The models’ combination is the conceptually a simple approach to

2.4 Federated Learning
One of the impediments for the usage of the distributed learning paradigm are the
regulations of data privacy, preventing the distributed data and computing resources
to be aggregated or directly shared among different regions or organizations for ma-
chine learning tasks [Liu et al., 2022b]. Federated learning (FL) emerged as privacy-
preserving distributed learning that allows multiple entities to collaboratively train a
ML model for a specific objective without divulging their individual input datasets.
This method utilizes data parallelization and asynchronous execution capabilities of
the SGD algorithm, enabling updates to be consolidated on a central server [Konečný
et al., 2016a, Konečný et al., 2016b, McMahan et al., 2016].
FL can be commonly defined as a machine learning setting for distributed learning,
whose goal is to train a high-quality centralized model while training data remains
distributed over several clients, also known as work nodes. The fundamental concept
involves establishing a central node or server to coordinate communication among all
participating nodes involved in the ML training. The orchestrator initiates SGD-based
MLmodelandmakesitaccessibletoallclients. Theseclientsthentraintheirindividual
versionsofthemodelusingtheirprivatedata[daSilveiraDibetal., 2024]. Theselection
of the SGD model is justified by its ability to be parallelized, executed asynchronously,
and updated on the central server [Shokri and Shmatikov, 2015]. After the local models
are concurrently trained within each client, only the models’ parameters or weights are
transmitted back to the central server. The central server then aggregates the weights
using the federated averaging algorithm, which will be explained in more details in
the next section [da Silveira Dib et al., 2024], as illustrated in Figure 2.18. This
approach preserves the privacy of the data because it is never exposed to external
audience, remaining inside the clients’ nodes. The FL proposal is intended to allow the
collaboration of different groups with similar problem to solve, regarding data usage,
and provide a powerful solution that would be, otherwise, very difficult to achieve.
Ideal problems for FL are the ones where centralizing the training data offers a disad-
vantage over distributed data, meaning that the data is privacy sensitive or the data
is too large to be easily aggregated [McMahan et al., 2016]. On the privacy aspect,

extensive empirical evaluation, considering five different model architectures and four
datasets. The main idea of their work is to explore a learning methodology that en-
ables users to collectively enjoy the advantages of shared models derived from extensive
data, all without the necessity of central data storage. The presented methodology in-
volved solving the learning task through a loosely connected federation of participating
devices, known as clients, which are orchestrated by a central server. Each client pos-
sesses a local training dataset that remains on the device without being transmitted to
the server. Instead, each client calculates an enhancement to the ongoing global model
maintained by the server, transmitting only this specific update. As these updates are
tailored to enhance the existing model, there is no need to retain them once they have
been applied [McMahan et al., 2016].
The authors identified the problem of training on decentralized data from mobile de-
vices as an important research direction. They also selected a straightforward and
practical algorithm that can be applied to the proposed setting and introduced an
improved version of FAA algorithm, which combines local SGD on each client with a
server that performs model averaging. Their experiment was performed in both image
classification and language modeling tasks and demonstrated that the FL method can
be made practical, as it trains high-quality models, compared with traditional options,
using relatively few rounds of communication.

5.2 Single and Ensemble Classifiers Comparison
Based on previous experiment results, although a classifier was clearly performing
better than the others, it is possible to have more than one option to build a machine
learning model that is able to produce satisfactory outputs in regard of the springback
prediction in U-Chanel forming process. One way to enhance the outputs of a model
without changing their hyperparameters is to combine several algorithms in one model.
The models’ results are averaged to produce, in theory, a better performing model.
Such method is calledensemble.
5.2.1 Ensemble Method
Ensemble methods are learning algorithms that construct a set of classifiers and then
classify new data points by taking a, weighted or not, vote of their predictions. In other
words, it is a set of classifiers whose individual decisions are combined in some way to
classify new examples, often providing more accurate classifier than the individual clas-
sifiers that make them up [Dietterich, 2000]. This is possible because several classifiers
can provide a reduction in the misclassification rate (error rate) of a single classifier
when they are aggregated, assuming they are accurate. As an example, consideringCL
as a classifier andx as a new sample to be predicted. When there is no diversity, if a
classification of the given sample made by one classifier, noted asCL 1(x), is incorrect,
the other classificationsCL 2(x), CL 3(x) and CLn(x) will also be incorrect, but if the
predictions are uncorrelated, thenCL 2(x),CL 3(x) andCLn(x) may be correct and the
voting system will correctly classifyx. Also, there are three more fundamental reasons
as to why the ensemble models may be better than single classifiers [Dietterich, 2000]:
75

Chapter 5. Selecting ML Classifiers for Defects Prediction
1. Statistical: Due to the lack of data, sometimes the classifiers can have a good
and similar, but not equal, approximation when predicting a samplex. By con-
structing an ensemble out of all these accurate classifiers, the algorithm can
average their predictions, mitigating the risk of selecting an incorrect classifier
and finding a better approximation value.
2. Computational: Many learning algorithms work by performing some form of
local search that may get stuck in local optima. In cases where there is enough
training data (so that the statistical problem is absent), it may still be very diffi-
cult computationally for the learning algorithm to correctly predict the unknown
label. An ensemble constructed by running the local search from many different
starting points may provide a better result than any of the individual classifier.
3. Representational: Some algorithms, assuming finite training samples, will ex-
plore only a finite set of solutions and they will stop searching when they find a
solution that fits the training data, which may not be the best.
Among the existing ensemble learning strategies, the two used in this experiment will
be described below.
Majority Voting
The majority voting ensemble is a machine learning model that aggregates predictions
from multiple other models. In classification scenarios, predictions are determined by
the majority vote of contributing models. Two approaches to majority vote prediction
exist for classification: hard voting and soft voting. Hard voting involves summing
predictions P for each class label and predicting the label with the most votes, where
P ≥ 0.5 (Figure 5.4), while soft voting involves summing predicted probabilities (or
probability-like scores) for each class label and predicting the label with the highest
probability [Brownlee, 2021].
Functioning as a meta-model, which is a model of models, majority voting ensembles
can be employed with any set of pre-trained machine learning models. These models
don’t need to be aware of their inclusion in the ensemble. Thus, a voting ensemble
can be applied to any combination of existing models or subsets for a given predictive
modeling task. Utilizing a voting ensemble is suitable when there are two or more

Stacking ensemble employs a meta-learning algorithm to adeptly combine predictions

6.1 Model’s Building Approach

ity to distribute the models’ training among several parties, alleviating the hardware
requirement for a given prediction task. Another advantage is the possibility to anal-
yse patterns of distributed datasets assuring their privacy, where in the standard ML
approach these datasets would have to be aggregated and stored in one, which is not
always possible because of the lack of data or privacy constraints, or both. There are
some ways to FL achieve its purpose, that are a largely categorized on the mode the
training data is partitioned. Based on the FL category, it can be named as horizontal
FL, vertical FL or federated transfer learning.
Vertical FL
Vertical FL proves to be well-suited for scenarios where data is segmented vertically
based on feature dimensions. In this context, all participant parties possess uniform
data, indicating that there is partial overlap in sample ID, but divergence exists in
the feature space. This setup allows for an enhancement of the model capacity by
leveraging the distinct features held by each party, thereby enriching the description
of samples through collaborative contribution from multiple sources [Li et al., 2020],
as shown in Figure 6.1.
Federated Transfer Learning
The federated transfer learning is best suited to scenarios involving data that lacks
both sample space and feature space overlaps. The primary challenge in this context is
the absence of data labels coupled with suboptimal data quality. The value of federated
transfer learning resides on facilitating the transfer of knowledge from one domain (the
source domain) to another domain (the target domain), ultimately enhancing learning
outcomes in the face of these data constraints [Li et al., 2020], demonstrated in Figure

7.1 FL Vulnerabilities
Modern machine learning systems face a spectrum of vulnerabilities, ranging from
non-malicious issues like errors in pre-processing pipelines and unreliable clients to
deliberate attacks targeting both training and deployment phases. Federated Learning,
due to its distributed nature and unique architecture, introduces new failure modes and
potential attack surfaces. The presence of privacy-preserving security mechanisms in
FL further complicates the task of detecting and mitigating these failures and attacks
[Kairouz et al., 2019].
Cyber-attacks on FL systems can take the form of external threats that manipulate the
training and inference pipelines, aiming to degrade the model performance by altering
its behaviour undesirably. These attacks can be broadly classified into untargeted at-
tacks, which seek to degrade overall model performance, and targeted attacks, which
aim to modify the model’s behaviour on specific samples while maintaining accuracy
on others. The attacks can be directed towards data, such as data poisoning, or the
model itself, encompassing model poisoning or model evasion. The two main categories
of attacks are training-time attacks, occurring during the model training process, and
inference-time attacks, impacting the model during real-time predictions or during
model evaluation [Kairouz et al., 2019]. Attackers may possess varying capabilities, in-
fluencing how they initiate attacks, observe model parameters, coordinate with others,
inject attacks during training, and adjust attack parameters over time. The subsequent
exploration will delve into the different ways attackers can introduce and adapt their
attacks [Kairouz et al., 2019].
Model Poisoning
A potent and inherent class of attacks is model poisoning attacks, where the attacker
has the ability to directly tamper with reports sent to the service provider. In feder-
ated settings, this manipulation can occur through the corruption of client updates,
either through direct manipulation or via a man-in-the-middle attack. In this context,
attackers may have control over specific clients, allowing them to directly influence the
outputs of these clients. This enables the attackers to strategically bias the learned
model towards their objectives [Kairouz et al., 2019].

Bagdasaryan et al. [Bagdasaryan et al., 2018] explores a potential attack strategy ap-
plicable by either an individual participant or multiple colluding participants within
federated learning. In this approach, a malicious participant can employ model re-
placement to insert backdoor functionality into the collective model. This means that
the classifier can be altered to assign a label chosen by the attacker to samples exhibit-
ing specific features. The newly introduced model maintains accuracy in the federated
learning task, yet the attacker gains control over the model’s performance in a backdoor
subtask of their choosing. For instance, a backdoored defect prediction classification
model may misclassify samples with certain features into an attacker-determined class.
The model replacement capitalizes on the observation that a participant in federated
learning can directly influence the weights of the joint model. This participant can train
in a manner beneficial to the attack by manipulating the weights of its local model ar-
bitrarily and/or incorporating evasion of potential defences into its loss function during
training.
Bhagoji et al. [Bhagoji et al., 2018] exploits the lack of transparency in the FL agent
updates and explores the possibility of an attacker controlling a small number of ma-
licious agents performing a model poisoning attack. The objective of this attack is
similar to the backdoor attack, that is to cause the jointly trained global model to
misclassify a set of chosen inputs with high confidence. The difference is that the at-
tack occurs on a model participating directly in the training, instead of inserting the
trained model only for the model averaging. Traditional poisoning attacks deal with
a malicious agent who poisons some fraction of the data in order to ensure that the
learned model satisfies some adversarial goal, but the authors considered instead an
agent who poisons the model updates it sends back to the server.
Data Poisoning
Data poisoning poses a more restrictive form of attack when compared to model poison-
ing. In this situation, the attacker does not have the capability to directly manipulate
the information sent to the central node. Instead, their influence is confined to manip-
ulating client data, involving actions like altering labels or specific features. Similar
to model poisoning, data poisoning can be employed for both targeted and untargeted
attacks. This type of attack gains significance when the attacker oversees the data

collection process at the periphery of the federated learning system but lacks the di-
rect capability to corrupt derived quantities within the learning system [Kairouz et al.,
2019].
Sun et al. [Sun et al., 2020], for instance, explores a data poisoning strategy in FL,
aiming for degrading the model performance, where three types of attacks may happen:
direct attacks, where the attacker can directly inject data to all the target nodes since a
door will be opened when collecting data;indirect attacks, where the target nodes are

target nodes in an indirect way;hybrid attack, where the attacker can inject poisoned

Additionally, Yerlikaya and Bahtiyar [Yerlikaya and Şerif Bahtiyar, 2022] analysed

performance results, but all of them sustained decreases for all evaluation metrics.
Model Evasion
In evasion attacks, an attacker may attempt to circumvent a deployed model by care-
fully manipulating samples that are fed into the model, usually by providing fraudulent
test inputs to compromise the model evaluation, configuring an inference-time attack.
Wang et al. [Wang et al., 2023] reveals the degree of vulnerability of existing FL-based
tasks have to such attacks. The authors developed an attack framework in which
model evasion perturbations are used to conduct model poisoning attacks in FL-based
networks. They assumed that a subset of the participating clients would intentionally
perturb their local datasets in an effort to force the corresponding local model to learn
a shifted distribution of the true received signals. The results shown that the training
convergence and classification performance of the global model is significantly degraded
as a result of the evasion perturbations induced on the subset of local clients.

7.2 Secure FL Methods
The confluence of challenges cited above may make robustness difficult to achieve in an
FL setup. However, there are several works that contributed with secure and privacy-
preserving FL approaches. The PEFL [Hao et al., 2020] was developed to address
model poisoning with noninteractive secure aggregation. This is achieved through
the use of homomorphic ciphertext in private gradients embedded into augmented
learning with an error term. The authors present a specific implementation wherein an
improved homomorphic encryption scheme is employed, eliminating the key-switching
operation and expanding the plaintext space. PEFL ensures privacy protection against
leakage from local gradients and shared parameters, even in the event of collusion
among multiple adversaries. Additionally, to enhance the privacy of training data, the
approach incorporates example-level differential privacy using a distributed Gaussian
mechanism. The performance evaluation highlights practical training accuracy, as well
as efficient computation and communication overhead.
Phong et al. (PPDL-AHE) [Phong et al., 2018] proposes a novel deep learning system
to protect the gradients over the honest-but-curious cloud server, when a server is con-
sidered safe but can access the private data, using additively homomorphic encryption.
All gradients are encrypted and stored on the cloud server. The additive homomorphic
property enables the computation across the gradients. The proposed system leaks no
information of participants to the honest-but-curious parameter server while achiev-
ing identical accuracy to a corresponding deep learning system trained over the joint
dataset of all participants. However, protecting the gradients against the cloud server
comes with the cost of increased communication between the learning participants and
the cloud server.
PP-MDL [Zhang et al., 2017] presents a new paradigm based on multiparty deep learn-
ing (MDL) ensuring that participating parties learn the global model (necessary for
MDL to function) only if enough local models are aggregated. No extra information
pertaining to individual local models is leaked in this process. This system provides
strong privacy assurance and desirable model utility simultaneously. It also offers an
intuitive handle for practitioners to balance model utility and training efficiency and
enables delicate control over communication and computational costs, thus optimizable

Bonawitz et al. [Bonawitz et al., 2017] (PSA) outlines an approach to advancing

7.3 Digital Envelopes
The primary objective of cryptography is to facilitate secure communication between
two parties in a potentially hostile network environment. When communicating over a
network is threatened by active attackers, the participants aim to ensure the confiden-
tiality and authenticity of the data they exchange. This is achieved by encrypting and
authenticating their transmissions using cryptographic algorithms. These algorithms
operate in conjunction with a key, which can be either a numeric or non-numeric ele-
ment employed to encrypt the message [Rashed et al., 2013].
In a secret-key encryption framework, known assymmetric cryptography, two parties
need to establish mutual agreement on a confidential key, ensuring its secrecy between
them (Figure 7.1). Additionally, they must rely on a trustworthy courier or another

during transmission, especially if the parties are situated in different locations. Any
unauthorized interception of the key during transit exposes the encrypted or authen-

this encryption method can accommodate a variable number of users, managing a large
number of users can present challenges. As the user count increases significantly, in
some cases it becomes impractical to handle all the associated keys. This can result
in shortcomings in the distribution and management of keys, introducing complexities
and potential security vulnerabilities. [Rashed et al., 2013].

Digital Signature
A digital signature system offers a method for users to sign messages, enabling subse-
quent verification by any party. In this scheme, each user generates a complementary
set of private and public keys. The signer, exclusively armed with its private key, can
generate a unique signature for a message. However, anyone can validate this signature
using the user’s public key. This process assures the verifier that the message remains
unaltered since its signing. Additionally, the signer cannot disavow the act of signing
the message, as only the signer holds the corresponding private key [Goldwasser and
Bellare, 2008].
If an attacker tries to issue a fraudulent update signed message to a user, when the
verifier attempts to verify the messages signature, it will find that this is an invalid
signature with respect to the signer public key. The signature will therefore be rejected,
even if the modified signature is only slightly different from a genuine one [Katz and
Lindell, 2014]. The DS scheme, within the public key framework, is defined as a triple
of probabilistic polynomial-time algorithms (G, S, V) such that [Katz and Lindell,

Conclusion and Future Work
The pressure over the manufacturing industry to deliver components faster, with high
quality and in quantities that only grow, while reducing the production cost by being
highly efficient, will increase due to the globalization and high demand. With the
introduction of industry 5.0 and smart manufacturing, many companies are reinventing
themselves to embrace the digital era and let this new paradigm guide them towards
having productions processes more robust and reliable. One possibility to be more
efficient in production is to prevent possible defects to occur. Of course, this can be
very expensive, require an astronomical domain knowledge and a lot of time for a
human being to perform such task, considering that this task is even possible to be
done.
Machine learning offers a powerful mechanism to predict defects in the manufacturing
environment by leveraging available data to build accurate defects prediction mod-
els. While it is evident that machine learning techniques are viable for application
in the manufacturing industry for defects prediction, it requires a substantial amount
of data for analysis and to generate predictive services. This requirement presents
another challenge, wherein only large companies typically possess the necessary data
to feed machine learning models for addressing production failures. However, simi-
lar challenges arise for SMEs, albeit they often lack sufficient data for analysis. A
collaboration between SMEs is possible, regarding data usage, but a straightforward
collaboration is not, due to data privacy and data protection policies.
The work in this thesis proposes a method to improve the collaboration among SMEs,

in order to allow them the possibility to become smart, and to take advantage of
what machine learning can provide to them. This thesis introduced a secure and pri-
vate framework to support this collaboration. This framework is composed of various
interconnected elements, each addressing a different issue or limitation of such collabo-
ration. The main contributions to support the research and development of mentioned
framework are: 1: A multi-stage methodology is proposed to develop predictive mod-
els, tailored to the unique characteristics of combining sheet metal forming process
information with ML techniques, 2: an implementation of a collaborative machine
learning for defects prediction in smart manufacturing, using distributed learning, and
3) the conceptualization and design of private and secure framework for a collaborative

These contributions represent significant advancements in secure and private machine
learning training, bridging existing gaps in the literature. These contributions provide
thorough and precisely outlined artifacts, guidelines, methodologies, and processes es-
sential for crafting collaborative defects predictors in manufacturing. Crucially, these
resources are tailored to address the unique characteristics of the problem domain and
the operational context in which the predictors will be utilized.
To demonstrate how the proposed contributions can be used in practice to develop ac-
curate, collaborative and secure defect predictors in manufacturing, a thorough experi-
mental evaluation was conducted. It comprised extensive ML model evaluation, consid-
ering different learning methods, several environments, such as, secure and non-secure,
and framework functionality and efficiency. The experimental evaluation overviewed
the use and relevance of each contribution. It assessed and thoroughly analysed the
viability and performance of several ML methods, for defect prediction, as well as per-
formance of the adaptation of a ML-based model into federated learning method to
also predict defects and evaluating the response of the secure federated learning frame-
work to external cyber-attacks, demonstrating that it is possible to develop accurate,
collaborative and secure defect predictors for smart systems.

with the data’s characteristics, as well as the technical requirements of the systems in

Future Work
The work developed in this thesis opened new research directions and issues for future
work, that are listed below in no particular order:
• Validate the observations and conclusions derived from assessing the
performance of the SecFL in a real-world production system: Ensur-
ing that the results obtained from various experimental evaluations hold true
in a production environment is a crucial step in promoting the investment and
development of SecFL. However, achieving this is no simple feat, as it requires es-
tablishing cooperation with a company or business where collaborative predictors
can be developed, deployed, and monitored.
• Evaluate the SecFL against different types of external attacks: As this
thesis solely focused on data poisoning protection, further investigation is war-
ranted to comprehensively understand the impact and efficacy of the current
framework against diverse threats. Given that SecFL is intended as a general
solution, a broad spectrum of scenarios can be explored, encompassing various
cybernetic threats.
• Create mechanisms to identify the external attacks: The SecFL operates
in a way where it is not required to know the type of the external attack, since it
is only necessary to verify if an attack occurred or not. If an attack is identified,

it is possible to take more sophisticated actions, such as, revert the corrupted
dataset to its original state instead of removing it from the training. However,
the challenge is to create such mechanisms while keeping the framework’s per-
formance similar, because, as stated previously in this thesis, a minimal change
can be impactful and very difficult to detect, making this a non-trivial task and
potentially impractical.
• Extend SecFL protection to in-memory processes: SecFL provides protec-
tion to the data in a hard drive and the network, which are the most vulnerable
environments. Although it is assumed that the operational system would provide
enough protection to all processes running only in memory, attack on memory
can happen, such as cold boot attacks [Bauer et al., 2016], and while it would
still be difficult to affect the data privacy, it may happen with more sophisti-
cated memory-based attacks in the future. To implement such extension would
be laborious as it requires deeper knowledge in cyber-security.
• Validate the usage of SecFL in smaller, less powerful single-board com-
puters: With the advance of the IIoT, it is common that some companies don’t
possess powerful computers but rather smaller devices, such as Raspberry Pi.
It is important to validate the usage of the SecFL on such devices in order to
expand its utilization.
• Evaluate the SecFL using a different base model for the federated
learning implementation: For the thesis and the data at hand, the artificial
neural network was the selected algorithm to serve as a base for the federated
learning implementation. Depending on the problem to be solved, the ANN
may not be the best model, in terms of performance, to achieve the desired
results. Therefore, it is important to verify the SecFL approach with different ML
algorithm as a base for the federated learning approach, since the FL approach
can be applied to other types of algorithms [Liu et al., 2019].
• Validate SecFL predicting different types of defects, to different types
of materials and different sheet metal forming processes: SecFL was
designed based on springback defect prediction in a U-Channel forming process
use case. In order to cover more scenarios within the manufacturing environment,
