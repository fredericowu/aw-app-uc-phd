Abstract
Spatial graphs play a crucial role across various domains, including biological
networks, urban planning, and technological infrastructures, by representing
complex relationships in a geometric context. This thesis explores the applic-
ation of deep learning techniques for optimising spatial graphs, with a partic-
ular focus on generating and optimising vascular networks. We introduce two
deep learning models to address the complex inherent properties of spatial
graph, either in their generation and optimisation. Through a multidisciplin-
ary approach that includes biology and artificial intelligence, we address the
challenge of modeling vascular networks, a critical component of biological
systems with direct implications for health and disease. We contribute with
the development of a reinforcement learning model for spatial graph optim-
isation, showcasing its efficacy in generating vascular networks that optimise
tissue irrigation. Additionally, we propose a generative deep learning model
capable of building vascular networks from existing real datasets, highlighting
the potential of data-driven approaches in this biological domain. We also
contribute with a novel multi-agent model for the simulation of vascular net-
work growth, exploring the complex dynamics of angiogenesis - the growth of
new blood vessels from existing ones - in the context of the adipose tissue.
The proposed work not only advances the state of the art in graph generation
and optimization but also serves as a groundwork for future applications in
biology and medicine.
Keywords: Network Optimisation, Reinforcement Learning, Generative
Models, Vascular Growth, Angiogenesis in Adipose Tissue

Resumo
Os grafos espaciais desempenham um papel crucial em diversos domínios, in-
cluindo redes biológicas, planeamento urbano e infraestruturas tecnológicas.
Através destas estruturas é possível representar relações complexas num con-
texto geométrico. Esta tese explora a aplicação de técnicas de aprendiza-
gem profunda para a otimização de grafos espaciais, com um foco particular
na geração e otimização de redes vasculares. São apresentados dois modelos
de aprendizagem profunda para abordar as propriedades complexas inerentes
aos grafos espaciais, tanto na sua geração como na otimização. Através de
uma abordagem multidisciplinar que inclui biologia e inteligência artificial,
enfrentamos o desafio de modelar redes vasculares, um componente crítico
dos sistemas biológicos, com implicações diretas para a saúde e associado a
diversas doenças. Contribuímos assim com o desenvolvimento de um modelo
de aprendizagem por reforço para a otimização de grafos espaciais, demon-
strando a sua eficácia na geração de redes vasculares que otimizam a irrigação
de um tecido. Propomos ainda um modelo generativo baseado em aprendiza-
gem profunda capaz de construir redes vasculares a partir de redes vasculares
existentes, destacando o potencial das abordagens baseadas em dados neste
domínio biológico. É ainda apresentado um novo modelo multi-agente para a
simulação do crescimento de redes vasculares, explorando as dinâmicas com-
plexas do processo de angiogénese - crescimento de novos vasos sanguíneos a
partir de vasos existentes - no contexto do tecido adiposo. O trabalho pro-
posto não só avança o estado da arte na geração e otimização de grafos, mas
também serve como base para futuras aplicações nas áreas da biologia e medi-
cina.

CHAPTER 1. INTRODUCTION
Graphs, and in particular spatial graphs, represent a fundamental structural
approach to understanding and modelling the complex networks present in
our lives. From urban infrastructure and transportation to biological struc-
tures and communication networks, graphs are a powerful tool to represent
and understand the complex interactions between the elements of a system.
Their optimisation is crucial for improving efficiency, resilience, and function-
ality across various sectors. Scientists and engineers have been using graph
theory to model and analyse complex systems for decades and, more recently,
exploring the potential of artificial intelligence, in particular deep learning, to
optimise and generate novel structures and patterns.
Inspired by the major importance and long-standing potential of these ap-
proaches, this thesis aims to advance the state of the art in the field of graph
generation. To support this goal, we bring to the table a very specific and
challenging problem of great importance in biology and medicine: the gener-
ation of graphs that represent vascular networks. These complex structures
are essential for some organisms, and their development and optimisation has
proven to be related to several pathologies and diseases.
1.1 Motivation
The core motivation of this research is settled in the well-established poten-
tial of deep learning approaches to generate and analyse spatial graphs [Zhu
et al., 2022, Guo and Zhao, 2022, Zhang et al., 2019a]. Regular methods
often struggle to handle the great complexity and dynamical nature of these
networks. Deep learning architectures offer an unprecedented capability to
model these systems, learn from their patterns, and predict their evolution.
They have opened many possibilities, exploring the recognisable data-centric
paradigm, where data collection is core in every domain. Therefore, this
massive presence of data can be seen as an opportunity: it opens the door
to new alternatives to traditional models, exploiting the ability to search and
learn patterns from vast datasets.
However, when applied to spatial graphs, deep learning comes along with a
set of challenges. The representation of spatial relationships and the encoding
of topological properties require suitable approaches in architecture modelling
and algorithm discovery. This thesis aims to address these challenges by
proposing novel deep learning models specifically designed for spatial graph

2.1.3 Reinforcement Learning
Reinforcement Learning (RL) is a subfield of Machine Learning that aims
to solve problems that involve sequential learning and decision-making under
uncertainty [Sutton and Barto, 2018]. As shown in Figure 2.1, RL comprises
two main entities - agent and environment - that communicate and interact
between them via observations, actions and rewards.
Figure 2.1: Reinforcement Learning Framework
An agent is an entity that interacts with the environment by making obser-
vations, executing actions and then being rewarded. Such a reward is usually
a scalar value, positive or negative, that instructs the agent on how good a
certain action was. The reward is a local property, reflecting the success of a
very specific action and not the overall performance of the agent.
In overview, an RL agent learns how to act greedily towards a specific goal
determined by the reward function. When in a given state, the agent decides
which action to take from a joint probability distributionP (s,a,s′) of mov-
ing to states′ after executing actiona in states. The goal of the agent is
to maximise the expected accumulated reward for all interactions with the
environment. Such a set of interactions is usually called a trajectory.

Model-Free vs Model-Based
An important distinction in RL is between model-free and model-based mod-
els. Model-free methods do not require a model of the environment and learn
directly from experience. They learn the optimal policy by explicitly inter-
acting with the environment. In this sense, the value of each state-action
pair is estimated and further used to select the next action. On the other
hand, model-based methods learn a representation of the environment. Such
representation is usually built from past experience or explicitly given by the
designer. The model is then used to predict the next state and reward given
the current state amd action.
When comparing both approaches, model-free methods are usually simpler
and easy to implement, as they do not require an explicit model of the en-
vironment. However, model-based methods are more sample efficient, as they
can learn from past experience and do not require to interact with the envir-
onment to learn the optimal policy.

Within model-free methods, two main approaches can be distinguished: value-
based and policy-based methods. Value-based methods learn a value function
that estimates how good a given state or state-action pair is. The value

2.3 Biological Background: Blood Vessels and Vascular
Networks
In addition to other components, the structure of blood vessels is mainly com-
posed of a inner mono-layer of endothelial cells (ECs), surrounded by pericytes
(that provide stability to the ECs), and, in larger vessles, by several layers of
smooth muscle cells (that control the vessel diameter and flow) [Mitchell
and Schoen, 2010]. ECs line the interior surface of blood vessels, having an
important function in the regulation of vascular tone, blood flow and blood
pressure. ECs also play a crucial role in the formation of new blood vessels.
In general terms, blood vessels are organised into three main types: arter-
ies, veins and capillaries. Arteries are blood vessels with thick walls which
transport oxygenated blood from the heart and distribute it to different parts
of the body. The smallest blood vessels are called capillaries, being respons-
ible for the exchange of oxygen, nutrients and other substances between the
blood and the surrounding tissues. They are usually composed of a single
layer of endothelial cells, facilitating the exchange of substances between the
blood and the tissues. Veins, on the other hand, are responsible for carrying
deoxygenated blood from the tissues back to the heart.
Branching vascular networks are organised in interesting patterns and usually
exhibit a fractal dimension [Lorthois and Cassot, 2010], with the same pattern
being repeated at different scales. Their structure and function is regulated by
various physiological and pathological factors, including blood pressure, blood
flow, inflammation and growth factors. Dysfunctional blood vessels and im-
paired vascular networks are associated with numerous diseases, underscoring
the great importance of understanding the mechanisms governing vascular
biology.

Tissue irrigation is an important aspect in vascular biology. It refers to the

3.1 Generative Models for Graphs
Graphs are used to represent objects and their connections in the real world
[West et al., 2001], such as social networks [Ohtsuki et al., 2006], citation
networks [Buneman et al., 2021], biological networks [Pavlopoulos et al.,

witnessed a significant amount of research on this subject, which may be
split into two broad categories: 1) analysis of patterns of existing graphs. 2)
using common properties of given graphs to generate synthetic ones. The
first category covers a wide range of subjects, such as node classification,
graphclassification, linkprediction, andcommunitydiscovery. Representation
learning approaches such as Graph Neural Networks have been widely used
to this intent [Waikhom and Patgiri, 2021]. The second category is more
connected to the finding of new algorithms capable of generating new graphs.
It has proven to have applications in various fields, including understanding
the dynamics in social networks [Grover et al., 2019, Tran et al., 2020, Wang
et al., 2018] and molecular generation.
The study of generative models for graphs has a long history, resulting in
well-known models such as random graphs, small-world models, scale-free,
stochastic block models, and bayesian network models. These methods pro-

been widely used in the past to model real-world graphs [Albert and Barabási,
2002, Leskovec et al., 2010]. However, these models are limited in their ability
to capture the complex structural characteristics of real-world graphs. In one
hand, due to their simplicity and handcrafted nature, they are often restricted
to describe more complex relationships and only capable of simulating a lim-
ited set of features of graphs (e.g., lack the heavy-tailed degree distribution
found in many real-world graphs). Second, the adoption of apriori assump-
tions prevents these classic approaches from exploring further application to
larger-scale domains where information on the graph properties is not always
acessible. Given these limitations, the development of generative models for
graphs has been a long-standing challenge in the field of machine learning and
graph mining, seeking to develop models that can learn from a given set of
graphs.

3.1.2 Sequential Methods
By gradually increasing the graph representation, sequential methods allow
the construction of complex graphs with a large number of nodes and edges.
Theses methods approach the graph generation process as a sequential decison
process, where nodes are generated one by one (or in groups), based on the
already existing sub-graph. These methods assume that a graphG can be

conditional joint distribution of all units. The methods found in the literature
have considered different representations for the unitui:
• Nodes: represent a graph as a sequence of nodes, generating nodes one-
by-one and its associated edges.
• Edges: represent a graph as a sequence of edges. At each iteration, an
edge and its two corresponding nodes are predicted.
• Motifs: represent a graph as a sequence of motifs, where a set nodes and
edges of each motif are generated at each step.
• Rules: generate a graph as a sequential set of rules. Each step yields a
rule/commands which produces a mutation on the graph.

3.1.3 One-Shot Methods
In contrast to sequential approaches, these methods learn to map the whole
graph to a latent representation. An entire graph is generated in a single
step, directly sampling from the probabilistic distribution. Considering that
a graph can be represented by either an adjacency matrix or an edge list, one-
shot methods can be classified into two categories: adjacency matrix-based
and edge list-based.
3.1.3.1 Adjacency Matrix-based
A significant number of the one-shot methods involve using models such as
variational auto-encoders (VAEs) or generative adversarial networks (GANs)
to generate novel graphs. The model receives a latent representation z ∼
p(z) as input and generates an adjacency matrix, the corresponding node
attributes and edge attributes. Both node attributes and edge attributes

From a different perspective, existing works have employed message-passing

3.1.3.2 Edge List-based
These methods employ a generative model that learn the probability distribu-

of this approach is that it is more efficient for large graphs, assuming that the
node set is already known. For example, Bojchevski et al. [Bojchevski et al.,

probability is based on a score matrix proportional to the frequency of each
edge appearing in a set of random walks. First, a GAN is utilised to learn the
random walks over the training graph and generate a set of random walks.
Then, a score matrix is built, where each entry represents the frequency of
each edge in the set of random walks. Finally, the score matrix is used to com-

the generated edge list.

3.1.4 Conditional Graph Generation
In the previous sections, we have discussed various approaches to generate
graphs with properties similar to the input graphs. However, in certain cases,
it is necessary to direct the generation process towards a particular pattern or
goal. For instance, if the aim is to generate molecules with specific properties,
the methods discussed earlier may not be adequate. Therefore, this section
focuses on exploring various techniques to condition the generation process
of specific objectives or properties. From the literature, we have identified
that the condition process can be performed at different levels: entire graph,
node/edge sequence or semantic context.
3.1.4.1 Conditioning on the Entire Graph
This class of methods aim to generate a novel graph conditioned on an existing
graph, and it is also known as graph-to-graph translation. The main motiva-
tion is to learn a mapping function that accepts a graph as input and generates
a new graph with similar properties. The main challenge is to find a suitable
representation for the graph that can be used as input to the model.
Some works have adopted an encoder-decoder. The encoder is responsible
for learning an abstract latent representation of the input graph, while the
decoder utilises this hidden information to generate a target graph [Gao et al.,
2018, Guo et al., 2022]. For example, Guo et. al. [Guo et al., 2022] proposed
a GAN-based model that consists of a graph translator and a conditional
graph discriminator. The graph translator includes an encoder and a decoder.
A graph convolutional network (GCN) is used to encode the input graph
into node-level embeddings, which are then passed to a graph deconvolution
network in order to generate the new graph.

3.1.4.2 Conditioning on the Sequence
The problem of generating a graph conditioning on the sequence of nodes
or edges aims to generate a graphGT conditioned by an input sequence X.
This approach is usually observed in other domains such as Natural Language
Processing (NLP) and time series processing.
Existing works employ a sequence-to-sequence models, such as the classical
RNN-based encoder-decoder models, to learn a graph representation based
on a sequence. Chen et. al. [Chen et al., 2018] proposed a model named
Sequence-to-Action. Given a sentenceX ={x1,x 2,...,x n}, the model learns
a sequence of actionsA ={a1,a 2,...,a m}, where each actionai is a graph
operation. The model is trained to maximise the likelihood of the generated
graphGT. The semantic context consists of nodes (entities or types) and edges
(relations), with a set of operations to be performed (e.g., count, sum, min).
To generate the semantic graph, four types of actions are available: add node,
add edge, operation function and the argument action. The resulting parse
tree is then represented as a sequence, and the sequence-to-graph problem is
converted into a sequence-to-sequence problem.
Another set of methods has handled the problem of Time Series Conditioned
Graph Generation. Yang et. al. [Yang et al., 2020] explores the application of
GANs and proposed a model of time series conditioned graph generation. The
generator consists in a variant of recurrent neural networks called simple re-
current units (SRU) to extract the temporal information from the time series.
Then, a simple neural network is used to generate the novel graph.
3.1.4.3 Conditioning on the Semantic Context
This last class of methods aims to generate a graph conditioned by a semantic
context. This context is usually represented by meta-features such as category,
label, modality or any other information that can be presented by a vectorC.
Different methods have been explored on how to incorporate this information
into the generation process.
Li et. al. [Li et al., 2018] concatenates the semantic context C into the
massing passing module of a GNN decoder. Specifically, the decoding process
follows a Markov process, where the graph is iteratively refined and updated
from the initialised graph.

3.2 Vascular Networks as Graphs
Vascular networks are naturally represented by graphs, where edges are vessels

The larger vessels in vascular networks form a familiar tree-like fractal hier-

and distributing it by successive generations of smaller vessels (with the ven-

very different at the scale of the smaller vessels. In fact, cappilaries networks
resemble more of a space-filling mesh than a branch tree.
Taking advantage of the representation of vascular networks as graphs, Shin
et. al. [Shin et al., 2019] proposed the first method to apply Graph Con-
volutional Networks to learn the graphical structure of blood vessels. The
proposed vessel graph network (VGN) applied a GCN together with a CNN
to convert vascular networks (e.g., DRIVE dataset) to graphical structures.
In a similar way, Mishra et. at. [Mishra et al., 2021] proposed a graph convo-
lutional neural network for artery/vein classification. The features extracted
from a CNN were used to generate a vessel topology and a Graph Neural Net-
work that allows the model to learn from local and global features of the graph
topology. In a different context, Chen et. al. [Chen et al., 2020] developed
a GNN approach to label the internal carotid artery from Magnetic Reson-

structures.
More recently, VesselGraph project [Paetzold et al., 2021] introduced a com-
prehensive dataset of whole brain vessels and benchmarked numerous state-
of-the-art graph learning algorithms on biologically relevant tasks, including
vessel prediction and vessel classification. This pioneering project marked
the first time a large dataset of high-resolution vessel graphs was made pub-
licly available. The dataset provides a new baseline for the development of
graph-based methods and the analysis of vascular networks, leveraging widely
employed open frameworks such as PyTorch Geometric and OGB. As will be
dicussed later, this dataset is the basis of part of the work presented in this
thesis and will be discussed further in detail in chapter 5.

3.4 Computational Models For Angiogenesis
Over the past decades, computational models have been created to simulate
biological systems in an effort to better understand the mechanisms driving
their dynamics [Brodland, 2015]. They have shown to be useful resources for
scientists working in the fields of medicine and biology, giving them insights
into intricate biological systems that may not be easily observable throughin
vitro/in vivoinvestigations. They clearly complement experimental methods
by offering a wider range of test settings. Nonetheless, experimental data is
still crucial for formulation and validation purposes.
Whereas some models provide a general overview of a real-life system, others
incorporate high levels of detail to better capture the system’s properties. For
example, in the context of angiogenesis, some models focus on the role of this
process in the growth of cancerous tissue, considering higher-level properties of
the system[Carmeliet and Jain, 2000, Hanahan and Weinberg, ]. Others may
specifically model a particular process, such as the interaction between tip
cells and the extracellular matrix, without explicitly considering its influence
on a specific context or pathology.
A study suggests three stages in modeling a biological system [Brodland,
2015]. The first stage involves defining the components of the system, which
depends on the desired level of detail. Such level of detail influences the
representation of the components, finding a middle ground between proximity
to reality and computational simplicity. The second stage involves defining the
interactions between the components, also considering the level of detail. The
— 44 —

third stage is model validation, which involves comparing the model’s results
with known scenarios through qualitative and quantitative inspection.
Heck et al. [Heck et al., 2015] categorised angiogenesis models into three cat-
egories. The first category focuses on tip cell migration, hypothesising that
it is responsible for the motion and morphology of new vessels. Tip cells
are located at the front of new sprouts and are responsible for extracellular
matrix degradation through secretion of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs),
thereby determining the path for other cells. The second category includes
models with an explicit distinction between endothelial cell phenotypes, and
also accounts for crucial processes such as proliferation, maturation, and cell
death.
Notably, none of the categories mentioned so far consider cell deformation
and elongation, as cells are typically modeled as rigid bodies that may change
in size as they grow before proliferating. The third category addresses this
aspect by modeling deformation due to mechanical forces from cell-cell and
cell-matrix interactions. This category can be seen as a refined version of the
second category, as it also takes into account the reshaping of stalk and tip
cells. In the following sub-sections, we will discuss the most relevant models
used to describe angiogenesis, following the categories proposed by Heck et
al. [Heck et al., 2015], as well as other models.
3.4.1 Tip Cell Migration Models
Several proposed models have considered the role of tip cells in governing
the movement and morphology of new sprouts. Early pioneers in this field
have used differential equations to track the migration of endothelial cells
when responding to chemical stimuli. For instance, Stokes et. al. [Stokes
et al., 1991] simulates a single cell moving on a 2D extra-cellular matrix,
induced by a chemical gradient. Subsquent works focused on simulating this
biological process in the context of tumor growth, where a cluster of tumor
cells induces the formation of blood vessels to provide nutrients and oxygen.
In this realm, a stochastic component drives the cell movement, biased by
the presence of chemical cues, such as VEGF. In a similar context, Tong
and Yuan et. al. [Tong and Yuan, 2001] studied this process in the eye of
animals, observing different vascular patterns depending on the concentration
of angiogenic factors.

4.1 Why Reinforcement Learning?
Reinforcement Learning offers an alternative perspective through which we
can address the challenge of vascular network generation. RL operates under
a top-down paradigm, emphasising the importance of achieving specific goals
or objectives, rather than detailing every microscopic interaction.
The fundamental principle of RL revolves around the concept of anagent
learning to make decisions that maximise cumulativerewards in an environ-
ment. Applied to vascular network generation, our agent learns to modify a
2D graph representing the network by strategically adding or removing edges,
with the objective of optimising tissue irrigation. This approach reduces the
complexity of modeling very specific details while aligning with our ultimate
goal of enhancing network functionality. By introducing RL into the field of
vascular biology, we anticipate the possibility of revolutionary advancements
in fields such as tissue engineering and regenerative medicine.

The problem is approached as a task of generating a graph, where a Q-

The Deep Q-Learning model adopts a systematic approach involving the
design of two distinct interrelated modules: the graph encoding module and
the Q-Value prediction module. The purpose of the graph encoding module
is to transform the complex structure of the graph into a concise, fixed-length
representation. It encapsulates the presence of edges from each node to its
predefined neighborhood. Throughout the simulation, all possible neighbors
are predefined and remain static in an auxiliary neighborhood map, ensuring
consistency in the encoding process.

a vector of equal length to the neighborhood size is initialised, with existing

the neighborhood structure within the encoded representation.

crucial during the subsequent end node selection step, enabling the model

retained within the encoder used in the start node selection stage to maintain
consistency and simplicity in the overall architecture.

4.5 Constant Blood Flow in Vessels
The preliminary results presented in Section 4.4 demonstrate the potential of
the proposed framework to generate vascular networks that efficiently irrigate
the tissue. However, the equations 4.3 and 4.4 start from the assumption that
the blood pressure is constant in the inlet nodes. Although an admissible
approximation, this assumption is not realistic. In fact, a constant blood flow
aligns more closely with the nature of blood flow, where the body regulates
the flow rate based on the metabolic demands and tissue needs rather than
strictly maintaining a constant pressure.
Motivated by this observation, a new irrigation function is proposed, which
considers a constant blood flow in every inlet node. The method described in
4.3.3 is slightly modified. Given the matrix representation of the pressures in
each node, the pressure in the inlet nodes is calculated similarly to any other
node (except for the outlet nodes, where the pressure is constant). Instead of
providing the pressure of the inlet nodes to the irrigation function, the flow
is provided instead. The pressures are then calculated using the Kirchhoff’s
laws.
Assuming this scenario, one can expect that the flow in the entire network will
be rather dependent on the topology of the network. For instance, abundant
branching when the input nodes have a constant pressure have small effect on
the flow in the network. However, when the input nodes have a constant flow,
the flow in each edge of the network is expected to be affected significantly by
the branching patterns. As an analogy, consider a water pipe with a constant
water flow. If the pipe branches, the flow in each branch will be reduced.
The reduction in the flow would not be so large if the pipe has a constant
pressure.
Although the representation of the vascular network remains the same, and
the apparent small change in the irrigation, we are under a significantly differ-
ent scenario. In fact, some preliminary experiments with this new irrigation
function showed how difficult it is in some scenarios to find networks that
fully irrigate the tissue given a combination of inlet flow and the minimum
threshold of oxygen concentration in the tissue.

forward problem in a biological perspective, where full irrigation is always
possible and required. The target is to achieve a certain percentage of irriga-
tion, which is not always 100%. It would somehow relax the problem and one
can expect that the agent would be able to learn faster.
We propose a new approach to the problem. Inspired by Curriculum Learn-
ing [Bengio et al., 2009] and Hindsight Experience Replay [Andrychowicz

fore diving into the details, both of these two approaches are briefly presen-
ted.

4.5.4.1 Curriculum Learning
Curriculum learning refers to a training strategy that involves gradually in-
creasing the complexity or difficulty of the learning task over time. Instead
of exposing the agent to a full and complex environment from the beginning,
curriculum learning follows a more structured approach, starting with simpler
scenarios and progressively introducing more challenging ones. This approach
aimstohelptheagentlearnmoreeffectivelybybuildingonpreviouslyacquired
skills. For example, in the domain of robotic manipulation, a reinforcement
learning agent might initially learn to grasp objects in a controlled and simpli-
fied environment. As the agent demonstrates proficiency in basic tasks, their
complexity could be gradually increased by introducing more diverse objects,
varying the object shapes, sizes, or textures, and eventually incorporating
more complex actions such as stacking or assembling objects.
The idea behind curriculum learning is inspired by the way humans learn,
where we often start with simpler tasks and gradually progress to more com-
plex ones. This methodology helps the RL agent to avoid being overwhelmed
by the complexity of the task and allows it to develop a more robust and
generalised policy by learning from simpler experiences first.
4.5.4.2 Hindsight Experience Replay
Hindsight Experience Replay (HER) is a technique to enhance learning from
failures by reframing them as successes. It is particularly useful in tasks with
sparse and binary rewards, where positive feedback is rare. HER works by
allowing the agent to learn from its mistakes, even if the initial goal was not
achieved.
Correlating HER with curriculum learning, we can view HER as a form of
dynamic curriculum adaptation during the training process. In a traditional
curriculum learning approach, the difficulty of the task is systematically in-
creased over time. HER introduces a unique twist by dynamically modifying
the goals of the agent based on its past experiences. For instance, in the
same context of robotic manipulation, if the initial goal is to place an object
in a specific location and the agent fails to achieve that, HER reframes the
failure by considering the actual outcome as a success. It then updates the
training data with this reframed experience, allowing the agent to learn from
both successful and unsuccessful attempts. Over time, the agent adapts its

curriculum dynamically, focusing on the aspects where it has struggled in the
past.
4.5.4.3 Turning Failure into Success
Inspired by the approaches presented in Sections 4.5.4.1 and 4.5.4.2, instead of
only considering full irrigation of the tissue as the goal, we retain intermediate
goals as successes. In this way, the agent is able to learn from its mistakes and
progressively improve its policy. The agent is exposed to a series of episodes,
where the goal is to achieve full irrigation of the tissue. If the agent fails to
achieve it, the episode is not considered a failure, but instead a success with
a different goal. This process is repeated and the policy is updated with new
experiences.
In fact, this approach does not merely learn from trial and error, but instead
make an effort to identify strategies that could be useful for another context
or task. Looking to how experience is being stored, the original experience
replay stores examples in the form of(s,a,r,s ′), wheres is the state,a is the
action, r is the reward ands′ is the next state. The question is: what if we
store something more? What if we could add fictious data, by imagining what
would had happen if the circumstance had been different? This is exactly the
idea behind the proposed method.
We can consider that the agent aims to reach a certain goalG in a given
episode when starting from the initial states0, but fails to do so. At the end
of the episode, the agent reaches statesT, and the trajectory is stored in the

CHAPTER 5. A DATA-DRIVEN APPROACH
We have previously explored the application of Reinforcement Learning —
a Markovian approach that optimises irrigation quality to guide the growth
of these essential biological structures. In this chapter, we pivot our focus
towards a different paradigm — a data-driven approach centered in the power
of imaging data to generate vascular networks with similar topology to those
found in nature.
5.1 Two Approaches, One Goal
Both the reinforcement learning approach presented in the preceding chapter
and the data-driven model we present here share a common assumption: vas-
cular networks are represented as graphs. However, their underlying method-
ologies and guiding principles diverge significantly:
• Reinforcement Learning for Irrigation Optimisation:In the pre-
vious chapter, we travelled through the dynamic world of reinforcement
learning, where our model was driven by the goal of optimising tissue
irrigation. Through a study of different reward mechanisms, it learned
to build efficient vascular networks, producing structures that maxim-
ise their utility in providing oxygen and nutrients, while minimising the
cost of building them.
• Data-Driven Generative Modelling:In this chapter, we introduce
an entirely distinct approach that capitalises on the existing imaging
data of vascular networks. We employ a sequential model trained on
real-world vascular structures. We pretend to build a model that learns
complex patterns, branching behaviors, and topology of vascular net-
works, allowing to generate synthetic networks.
In the sections that follow, we walk through the details of this data-driven
model, exploring its potential to reshape our understanding and generation of
vascular networks.
5.2 Proposed Model
Similar to the reinforcement learning model presented in Chapter 4, the pro-
posed model approaches the generation of vascular networks as a sequential
graph generation problem. In this case, the model explores existing data of
spatial graphs in a three-dimensional space. The sequential graph generative

process is based on the idea that graphs can be expanded from a given ini-
tial structure. The expansion process is performed iteratively, where at each
iteration, a sequence of moves from a given node is executed. The moves are
conditioned on the current state of the graph: the node to be expanded and
the surrounding nodes.
In a nutshell, our proposed model adopts a supervised learning framework.
Instead of treating the coordinates of graph nodes as continuous variables,
we discretise them into a predetermined number of categories/classes. This
discretisation strategy serves to limit the potential values for each coordin-
ate to specific discrete options, effectively reducing the output space. We
believe this approach can enhance the capacity of model to learn. The multi-
class approach we employ introduces distinct boundaries between potential
coordinates. This feature is advantageous as it helps prevent the generation
of unrealistic vascular structures. It is important to note that the choice of the
number of classes is arbitrary, and greater granularity in structure generation
can be achieved with a higher number of classes. However, it is important to
recognise that this choice is constrained by the resolution of the input data,
as it defines the practical limits of discretisation.
5.2.1 Graph Generation as a Sequential Process
An iterative methodology is conducted for the generation of novel graphs, with
a focus on incorporating structural patterns observed in actual graphs. Graph
generation is approached as a sequence generation problem, where a series of
actions is produced to construct a graph. During each iteration, two distinct
node types are taken into account:open nodesand closed nodes. Open nodes
are eligible for expansion, whereas closed nodes remain unchanged (although
they can be connected to/by other nodes). At a given iteration, from the
set of open nodes, one node is randomly selected’ as the active node and
expanded.

The proposed method employs a sequence-to-sequence model, comprising two
recurrent neural networks: an encoder and a decoder (refer to Figure 5.1). The
encoder processes each path as a sequence, representing the relative positions
of consecutive nodes. It then produces a compressed representation for each
path, derived from the final hidden state of the encoder. Subsequently, an
aggregation operator (such as sum) is applied to all paths, resulting in a
single context vector.
The decoder initialises its hidden state using this context vector and proceeds
to generate a sequence of three-dimensional moves, guiding the expansion
to the next ‘Nout_nodes‘ nodes connected to the active node. This iterative
process continues until a predefined maximum number of steps is reached. In
Figure 5.1, you can observe an illustrative example of this procedure, where
a maximum path length of Lmax = 6 and a maximum number of paths,
Nin_paths = 3, are specified. In this case, Node1 serves as the active node,
and three paths are sampled from it (indicated by the dashed lines).
5.2.2 Sampling and encoding paths
We initiate the process by sampling a maximum ofNin_paths paths, all starting
from the current active node. This operation can be compared to obtaining
a partial view of the graph structure centered around this particular node.

Formally, consider a pathp = (v1,v 2,...,v L) with a lengthL≤Lmax. Here,
v1 denotes the active node, and vL represents the final node of the path.
We define the relative position between consecutive nodes,vi and vi+1, as
di = cvi+1−cvi, where cvi corresponds to the 3-dimensional coordinates of
node vi.
Subsequently, the encoder receives a sequence of relative positions d =
(d1,d 2,...,d L) as input. In this sequence,d1 is the relative position between
the active node (v1) and the immediate subsequent node (v2) in the path.
It is noteworthy that the final relative position,dL, is consistently zero, and
each di represents a transition fromvi to vi+1. It is also important to note
that the lengths of these relative positions may vary, depending on the specific
path’s length (L). However, this length depends on the constraint set by the
maximum path lengthLmax.
Upon the completion of input path sampling and the conversion of paths into
relative positions, each path is encoded using a recurrent neural network. The
chosen encoder is a bidirectional gated rectified unit network (Bi-GRU), with
the hidden state initialised as a zero vector. The final hidden states of indi-
vidual paths are then combined to generate a condensed path context. This
encoding process is applied to every path, resulting in a collection of encoded
paths enc_paths = (ep1,ep 2,...,ep Nin_paths), whereepi means a vector rep-
resenting the ultimate hidden state for thei-th path. In order to obtain a
single context that can initialise the decoder, all encoded paths are merged
into a unified context vectorc. This context vector is computed as the sum
of all encoded paths.
5.2.3 Decoding Follow-up Nodes
The final step of the generation process consists in predicting a set of follow-
up nodes connected to the active node. In this sense, given a context vector
that aggregates the information of all paths that start from the active node,
the decoder network predicts the moves that produce a maximum ofNout_nodes
follow-up nodes. Each move can either produce a new node or establish a loop
with an already existing node. The decoder is a bidirectional GRU network,
where the hidden state is initialised with the context vectorc. The decoder is
trained to predict a set ofNout_nodes nodes, whereNout_nodes is the maximum
number of nodes that can be added to the graph at each step.Nout_nodes is

5.2.4 Generating Training Data from Graph
Prior to training the model, a pre-processing stage is conducted to obtain
the sequential steps that produce the topological properties of the training
graph. It consists in executing the graph generation process described in
Sections 5.2.2 and 5.2.3 on the training graph. The process is repeated for
each node of the graph, resulting in a set of training examples. Each training
example consists of a set of paths starting from a given node, and the set of
follow-up nodes connected to it. The model is trained to predict the follow-up
nodes based on the current local topology, which is represented by the paths
starting from the active node.
Given that the proposed model operates on spatial graphs, each node contains
informationaboutits3-dimensionalcoordinates. Inthissense, themodelmust
be able to ingest the information of the paths in a 3-dimensional Euclidean
space and predict the “moves” that generate the coordinates of the follow-up
nodes. One approach would be to consider the coordinates in a continuous
space, treating the problem as a regression task. Nonetheless, we encode the
coordinates into a discrete space, treating the problem as a classification task.
The main reason for this choice is that it constrains the model to generate co-
ordinates within a given range, also reducing the space of solutions. Formally,
given an arbitrary number ofC classes, the maximum and minimum values
of each coordinate in the training graph are computed and scaled to a range
[0,C ] (rounding to the nearest integer). As we are working with “moves”
in a 3-dimensional space, both negative and positive values are considered.
In it, the class0 corresponds to the lowest value of the coordinate, and the
class C corresponds to the highest value. The remaining classes are distrib-
uted linearly between the minimum and maximum values, where classC/2
corresponds to the value0.0. For example, considering thatC = 10 and the
coordinate values range between[−1, 1], the value−1 is encoded to class0,
the value 0 is encoded to class5 and the value1 is encoded to class10. We
consider that this representation provides a good trade-off between the com-
plexity of the model and the expressiveness of the 3-dimensional euclidean
space.

increased. However, the number of combinations of paths is bounded, being
dependent on the maximum number of pathsNin_paths, the maximum path
length Lmax and the topology of the graph. In this sense, the number of
iterations T is a hyperparameter of the model and is usually set to a value
that is high enough to generate a diverse set of training examples, but low
enough to avoid overfitting.

5.2.6 Model Inference
After training both the encoder and the decoder networks, the model is able to
generate new graphs. The generation process is similar to the one described
in Sections 5.2.2 and 5.2.3, with the difference that we start from a seed
graph Gseed. The seed graph is usually a sub-graph of the original dataset,
although it can also be a randomly generated graph. We consider that the
seed graphGseed is composed of closed and open nodes. From the open nodes,
at each iteration a single one is randomly selected as the active node. The
generation process is then applied to the active node, resulting in a set of
follow-up moves that can either produce new nodes or establish a new edge
with already existing nodes. The process is repeated until no more active
nodes are available or a maximum number of iterations is reached.
5.2.6.1 Establishing Connections to Existing Nodes
An important aspect of the generation process is the establishment of con-
nections to already existing nodes. In fact, graphs and spatial graphs are
usually composed of cycles, making it necessary to implement a mechanism
that supports this topological property. In this sense, after predicting the
moves to establish the follow-up nodes, a parameterR determines the dis-
tance threshold to establish a connection between the active node and an
already existing node. If this distance is below the threshold, a new is not
added, but instead a connection to the nearest node is established. As will
be discussed, this mechanism has a significant impact on the topology of the
generated graphs.

5.5.1 Evaluation Metrics
Graph similarity is a critical aspect when assessing the quality and perform-
ance of the proposed generative model. It enables us to determine how well
the generated graphs align with the characteristics, topology, and connectiv-
ity patterns found in the target or ground truth graphs. To comprehensively
evaluate the quality of the generated structures and make meaningful com-
parisons with the ground truth, we employ a range of metrics, each capturing
different aspects of graph similarity and structural fidelity:
1. Degree Distribution and Degree Rank Comparison: This metric
compares the distribution of node degrees in the generated graph to
that of the ground truth. Degree distribution refers to the frequency of
different node degrees (the number of edges incident on a node) across
the entire graph. A high similarity in degree distributions indicates that
the generative model is preserving the relative number of nodes with
various degrees.
2. Distance Between Neighboring Nodes Comparison: In this met-
ric, we assess the average distance between neighboring nodes within the
generated graph and compare it to the ground truth. Neighboring nodes
are those connected by an edge. A smaller average distance in the gener-
ated graph suggests that it retains the proximity and local connectivity
patterns observed in the real graphs.
3. Graph Embedding Comparison: To capture global structural in-
formation, we compute the Euclidean distance between the graph em-
beddings of the generated graph and those of the ground truth. Graph
embeddings represent a compact, numerical representation of the en-
tire graph. We obtain these embeddings using the ProNE algorithm, as
described in [Zhang et al., 2019b]. A smaller Euclidean distance indic-
ates that the generative model is successful in reproducing the overall
structural characteristics of the real graphs.

4. Density Comparison: This metric involves comparing the density of
the generated graph to that of the ground truth. Graph density is a
measure of how interconnected the nodes are in a graph. For undirec-

6.1.2 Angiogenesis in the Adipoose Tissue: Biological Over-
view
Before proceeding to the architectural components of the model, it is essen-
tial to revisit the biological aspects of angiogenesis, particularly within the
context of adipose tissue. Two critical components can be highlighted: en-
dothelial cells (ECs) and the extracellular matrix (ECM). They play a pivotal
role in the development of blood vessels. Blood vessels are primarily lined with
ECs, which are central to the growth and repair of these vascular structures.
An overview of the sprouting angiogenesis is described in Figure 6.2. This
process is initiated by external signals, subsequently inducing EC chemotaxis
— the cellular movement towards chemical signals. It encompasses the migra-
tion of ECs driven by chemical cues, thereby triggering phenotypic changes.
On the other hand, the ECM represents a complex network of proteins and
carbohydrates that provides support to cells within tissues. Collectively, ECs
and the ECM are integral components in the complex processes responsible
for the formation and maintenance of blood vessels.
One of the key structural features of the ECs leading a growing sprout is the
— 115 —

CHAPTER 6. A BOTTOM-UP AGENT-BASED MODEL
presence of thin extensions known as filopodia. Filopodia serve as sensory
antennae for cells, allowing them to sense and respond to their surrounding
environment. They are involved in detecting chemical cues, physical cues,
and other signals from nearby cells or the extracellular matrix. These ECs
that exhibit extended filopodia are referred to as "tip cells," displaying an in-
vasive behavior as they navigate through the ECM. This invasive behavior is
facilitated by tip ECs producing metalloproteinases (MMPs), which degrade
the ECM [John and Tuszynski, 2001]. The MMPs are a family of enzymes
that degrade the components of the ECM, creating paths for cellular migra-
tion.
In contrast, “stalk cells” are composed by fewer filopodia and exhibit less in-
vasive behavior, primarily following the lead of tip cells and displaying higher
proliferation rates. The formation of new vessel loops occurs through a pro-
cess known as “anastomosis” [Moreira-Soares et al., 2018, Potente et al., 2011],
which takes place when a tip cell from one vessel connects with another cell
from a neighboring vessel. This results in the establishment of blood flow
within the new loop, leading to stability as the levels of pro-angiogenic factors
decrease. It is worth noting that ECs change dynamically between these two
different phenotypes of tip and stalk cells, leading to a competitive dynamic
during this process [Jakobsson et al., 2010]. This competition is mediated
through intercellular communication, regulated by negative feedback mech-
anisms [Suchting et al., 2007]. Initially, the response to angiogenic factors,
primarily vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), is similar among ECs,
as they are all stimulated to become tip cells. However, tip cells express lower
levels of Notch signaling, while stalk cells express higher levels. Notch signal-
ing potentially inhibits the tip cell phenotype, and more specifically, tip cells
have membrane proteins that interact with Notch receptors on neighboring
cells, thereby suppressing their tip cell characteristics.
6.1.2.1 Obesity and Adipose Tissue
Obesity has become a global epidemic affecting an increasing number of people
worldwide [Doak and Popkin, 2017]. It is associated with various health dis-
orders, including metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular diseases, and type-2 dia-
betes [Consultation, 2000]. In most cases, obesity is linked to a significant
increase in calorie intake, leading to the expansion of adipose tissue, which
is the primary structure for storing excess energy. Adipose tissue is then in
— 116 —

CHAPTER 6. A BOTTOM-UP AGENT-BASED MODEL
continuous remodeling, playing a vital role in maintaining glucose levels. The
excess of energy is primarily stored in adipocytes, the predominant cell type
in adipose tissue, which typically supplies energy to other cells [Rodrigues
et al., 2014]. Like other cells, adipocytes require oxygen and nutrients for
their survival and proper function. These essential components are transpor-
ted through the bloodstream, emphasising the critical role of a well-structured
vasculature in providing nutrients and oxygen to tissues.
To meet the changing demands of adipose tissue due to variations in calorie in-
take, the aforementioned dynamic process called angiogenesis comes into play.
Duringtheprogressionofobesity, adipocytessufferhypertrophy, meaningthey
increase in size due to calorie uptake. This hypertrophy leads to two hypo-
theses regarding the promotion of angiogenesis in adipose tissue [Guilherme
et al., 2008]. One hypothesis suggests that angiogenic factors are released
by hypertrophic or proliferating adipocytes, similar to what is observed in
tumor vascularisation. As adipocytes grow and proliferate, they can become
hypoxic (oxygen-deprived), prompting the release of pro-angiogenic factors.
These growth factors bind to receptors on the surface of endothelial cells,
activating their internal signaling pathways and promoting cell migration.
However, studies have shown that VEGF-induced angiogenesis alone may not
be sufficient to ensure efficient vascularisation during adipose tissue growth,
suggesting the involvement of other factors. Another paradigm proposes that
angiogenesis begins in the early stages of adipocyte hypertrophy, indicating
that adipocytes have the ability to sense the need for a proper blood sup-
ply. This leads to the release of angiogenic factors before hypertrophy reaches
its peak. In vitro experiments with pre-adipocytes have demonstrated that
capillary network remodeling occurs before adipocyte formation. Inhibiting
pro-angiogenic signals in the early stages of adipocyte development signific-
antly impacts overall adipose tissue growth, supporting this paradigm. Addi-
tionally, studies suggest the existence of cells on the vessel surface that have
the potential to become either endothelial or adipocyte-like cells, indicating a
novel paradigm of cell-cell interactions involved in vessel development within
adipose tissue.
From a different perspective, some studies have focused on the role of hypoxia-
induced factors (HIF) in mediating angiogenesis in adipose tissue, comparing
it to the same process in tumor growth [Hausman and Richardson, 2004, Sun
et al., 2011]. HIFs are a family of transcription factors that play a crucial role
— 117 —

CHAPTER 6. A BOTTOM-UP AGENT-BASED MODEL
in the cellular response to low oxygen levels, being responsible for regulating
the expression of genes that help cells to adapt to and survive in low-oxygen
environments. While tumors generate zones of hypoxia that upregulate HIF
signaling and promote angiogenesis, the adipose tissue responds differently
under HIF expression. Instead of upregulating pro-angiogenic proteins, in-
flammation, cell death, and macrophage infiltration are observed in adipose
tissue. This suggests that the angiogenesis process in adipose tissues differs
from what occurs in tumor-induced angiogenesis, highlighting the need for a
better understanding of the mechanisms involved in adipose tissue vascular-
isation.
The presence of adipocytes in adipose tissue is definitely is a key factor that
differentiates it from other tissues. In contrast to tumor-induced angiogen-
esis, the adipose tissue mainly consists of adipocytes that interact with blood
vessels, affecting vascular growth. Although the mechanics of isolated adipo-
cytes are not well understood, experimental studies have revealed interesting
mechanical properties of adipose tissue. For example, adipose tissue responds
to compression in a specific manner, and a hypothetical model involving a
closed-cell foam with an incompressible viscous fluid has been proposed to de-
scribe the mechanisms governing this tissue [Alkhouli et al., 2013]. Moreover,
studies have investigated the mechanical properties of adipocytes in terms of
stiffness, revealing that different maturation stages of adipocytes exhibit dis-
tinct stiffness levels. As adipocytes grow larger, their stiffness increases, with
lipid droplet interactions playing a significant role in this process [Shoham
et al., 2014].
In summary, the key question is centered around understanding the con-
sequences of promoting or inhibiting angiogenesis in adipose tissue. Some
evidence from biological experiments suggests that inefficient vascular adapt-
ation can delay adipose tissue growth. In such cases, hypoxia stress generated
by adipocytes leads to inflammation and insulin resistance. Conversely, in
some cases upregulation of VEGF can improve vascularisation, reduce inflam-
mation, and reverse insulin resistance.

mechanism in the context of adipose tissue provides a new perspective and
potential insights into the development of obesity and type-2 diabetes. Such
agent-based modeling approach presented a bottom-up perspective, offering
a granular view of the individual components and their interactions within
a vascular network. We have demonstrated its potential and importance,
establishing a groundwork for in-depth studies of the angiogenesis process and
its role in the development of the adipose tissue. Moreover, the integration
of a physics engine introduces a novel mechanical perspective, providing an
explicit representation of the forces that drive the angiogenesis process, and
the interactions between the different components of the tissue.
The nature of the research presented in this dissertation is computationally

resources. Although an effort has been made to optimise the code and the

All efforts were made to have available as much resources as possible, reason

CHAPTER 7. CONCLUSION
7.1 Future Work
Even though the results of this research shed light on the potential of the
proposed methodologies, there are still many aspects to be addressed:
• Enhancing Model Generalisation:further research should focus on
generalising the models to other types of spatial graphs beyond vascular
networks. This should include exploring different graph structures and
incorporating a broader range of features and properties.
• Extend the Complexity of the Environment:we can in the fu-
ture explore more complex environments, with a wider range of initial
conditions and more complex objectives. We are aware that this would
require much more computational resources.
• Integrate Other Datasets:the current data-driven model is trained
on a single dataset obtained from a specific type of tissue. Future work
could explore the integration of other datasets, to enhance the capacity
of the model to generalise and common patterns across different types of
tissue. Unfortunately, the lack of publicly available datasets for vascular
networks makes this task challenging.
• Transition from Bi-GRU to Transformer Architecture: while
the Bi-GRU model has proven effective for sequential data processing,
recent advances in transformer models have demonstrated superior per-
formance in capturing long-range dependencies and parallelising com-
putations. By transitioning to a transformer architecture, future work
could benefit from its ability to handle larger sequences and complex
relationships more efficiently, potentially leading to improved accuracy
and scalability in generating synthetic vascular networks.
• Enhance the Agent-based Model:the agent-based model could be
further enhanced by integrating more complex cellular behaviors and
interactions. Thiswouldprovideamoredetailedviewoftheangiogenesis
process and its role in the development of adipose tissue and diseases
such as type-2 diabetes.
